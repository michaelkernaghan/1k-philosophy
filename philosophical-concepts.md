# The 1,000 Most Important Philosophical Concepts

Indexed, ordered and summarized. Part I groups the concepts by branch of philosophy, and within each branch they run from the most to the less central. Part II is an alphabetical index giving each concept's entry number.

The selection and the within-branch order are editorial judgments, not a measured ranking. Concepts that belong to several branches appear once, in the branch where they are most at home.

# Part I: Concepts by Branch


## 1. Metaphysics and Ontology

**1. Being.** The most general question of what it is for anything to exist at all; Parmenides, Aristotle and Heidegger each made it central, asking what "is" means across all that is.

**2. Substance.** The underlying bearer of properties that persists through change; for Aristotle the primary reality, for Descartes and Spinoza what depends on nothing else, for Locke an unknowable substratum.

**3. Essence.** What a thing is in itself, those features without which it would not be the thing it is, as opposed to its merely accidental traits.

**4. Existence.** Whether and how anything is actual; Kant argued it is not a real predicate, and Quine held that to be is to be the value of a bound variable.

**5. Universals.** Properties or kinds, such as redness, that many particular things seem to share; the debate is whether they exist beyond their instances.

**6. Particulars.** Individual, unrepeatable things located in space and time, contrasted with the universals they instantiate.

**7. Nominalism.** The view that only particulars exist and that general terms name no shared entities, only similar things or the words we apply to them.

**8. Realism about Universals.** The view that universals exist, either apart from their instances (Plato) or only in them (Aristotle, Armstrong).

**9. Platonic Forms.** Plato's eternal, perfect, non-physical patterns, such as the Good and the Beautiful, of which sensible things are imperfect copies.

**10. Matter.** The stuff or potential from which things are made; in Aristotle it is what takes form, in modern thought what physics describes.

**11. Hylomorphism.** Aristotle's view that every natural thing is a compound of matter and form, with the form making the matter the kind of thing it is.

**12. Causation.** The relation by which one event or state brings about another; analyses include regularity, counterfactual dependence, and productive power.

**13. Four Causes.** Aristotle's material, formal, efficient and final causes, four distinct ways of answering "why is this thing as it is?"

**14. Teleology.** Explanation by purpose or end; Aristotle saw ends in nature, while modern science generally excludes final causes outside agency.

**15. Potentiality and Actuality.** Aristotle's pair for what a thing can become versus what it currently is, used to explain change without making something come from nothing.

**16. Change.** The problem of how a thing can become different yet remain itself; Parmenides denied it and Heraclitus made it fundamental.

**17. Becoming.** Reality understood as ceaseless flux rather than fixed being, associated with Heraclitus, Hegel and Whitehead.

**18. Identity.** The relation each thing bears only to itself; philosophers ask what makes a thing one and the same over time and across descriptions.

**19. Identity of Indiscernibles.** Leibniz's principle that no two distinct things share all their properties; its converse, the indiscernibility of identicals, is uncontroversial.

**20. Leibniz's Law.** If a and b are identical, whatever is true of a is true of b; it is the standard test for ruling out claimed identities.

**21. Persistence.** How an object exists across time, either by being wholly present at each moment (endurance) or by having temporal parts (perdurance).

**22. Endurantism.** The view that an ordinary object is wholly present at every moment of its existence and has no temporal parts.

**23. Perdurantism.** The view that objects are four-dimensional wholes extended in time, with each moment a temporal part, as developed by Lewis and Sider.

**24. Time.** The dimension of before and after; Augustine and McTaggart asked whether it is real, and physics and metaphysics dispute its direction and flow.

**25. A-Theory of Time.** The view that the passage of time and the distinction of past, present and future are objective features of reality.

**26. Presentism.** The A-theory that only present things exist, so the past is gone and the future is not yet.

**27. B-Theory of Time.** The view that events are ordered only by earlier-than and later-than, and that the flow of time is an illusion of perspective.

**28. Eternalism.** The view that past, present and future events all exist equally, as in the block universe suggested by relativity.

**29. Growing Block Universe.** The view that the past and present exist but the future does not, so reality grows as time passes.

**30. McTaggart's Paradox.** The argument that time is unreal because the A-series is contradictory and the B-series alone cannot give change.

**31. Space.** The arena of spatial relations; the question is whether it is a thing, a container, or just the relations among bodies.

**32. Substantivalism.** The view that space or spacetime is a real entity existing independently of the objects in it, as Newton held.

**33. Relationalism.** The view that space and time are nothing over and above relations among objects and events, as Leibniz argued.

**34. Possibility and Necessity.** The modal notions of what could be and what must be, which give metaphysics its distinction between the contingent and the unavoidable.

**35. Possible Worlds.** Complete ways things might have been, used since Leibniz and Kripke to analyse necessity, possibility and counterfactuals.

**36. Modal Realism.** David Lewis's thesis that possible worlds are as real as our own, concrete and causally isolated from one another.

**37. Actualism.** The view that only actual things exist, so talk of merely possible entities must be reduced to something actual.

**38. Contingency.** The status of what is true but might have been false; Leibniz and Aquinas used it to argue for a necessary ground.

**39. Necessary Being.** A being that cannot fail to exist, invoked in cosmological arguments as the explanation of contingent things.

**40. Principle of Sufficient Reason.** Leibniz's principle that everything has a reason why it is so rather than otherwise.

**41. Essentialism.** The view that some properties are essential to an object, so it could not exist without them.

**42. Haecceity.** The "thisness" of an individual, the non-qualitative property that makes it this very thing and no other, a notion due to Duns Scotus.

**43. Individuation.** The question of what makes one thing a single, distinct individual, whether matter, form, location or bare particularity.

**44. Quiddity.** The "whatness" of a thing, its essence as answering the question what it is, a term of scholastic philosophy.

**45. Monism.** The view that reality is ultimately one kind of thing, or even one thing, as in Parmenides, Spinoza and Advaita.

**46. Pluralism.** The view that reality comprises many fundamentally distinct entities or kinds, with no single underlying one.

**47. Idealism.** The view that reality is fundamentally mental or dependent on mind, whether Berkeley's ideas, Kant's transcendental form, or Hegel's Spirit.

**48. Materialism.** The view that everything that exists is matter or depends on matter, a thesis traceable to Democritus and Lucretius.

**49. Naturalism.** The view that reality is exhausted by the natural world studied by science, with no supernatural order beyond it.

**50. Emergence.** The idea that complex systems have properties that are not predictable from, or reducible to, those of their parts.

**51. Supervenience.** A dependence relation on which there can be no difference in one domain, such as the mental or moral, without a difference in another.

**52. Reduction.** Explaining one domain, a theory or kind of entity, wholly in terms of another more basic domain.

**53. Grounding.** A relation of metaphysical explanation by which one fact holds in virtue of another, such as the fact that a set exists in virtue of its members.

**54. Fundamentality.** The question of which entities or facts are basic and which are derivative, structuring all of metaphysics.

**55. Mereology.** The formal theory of parts and wholes, asking when objects compose a further object and how parthood behaves.

**56. Composition.** Van Inwagen's Special Composition Question: under what conditions do several things add up to a single object?

**57. Vagueness.** The fact that many predicates lack sharp boundaries; the issue is whether this is a defect of language, knowledge or reality.

**58. Ship of Theseus.** A puzzle about identity over time: if every plank of a ship is replaced, and the old planks reassembled, which ship is the original?

**59. Properties.** The qualities and relations things have; the debate is whether they are universals, sets, tropes or something else.

**60. Tropes.** Particularized properties, such as this particular redness of this apple, proposed as the basic building blocks of reality.

**61. Bundle Theory.** The view, taken from Hume, that an object is nothing but a collection of properties with no substratum beyond them.

**62. Natural Kinds.** Categories that carve nature at its joints, such as gold or tiger, as opposed to arbitrary groupings.

**63. Dispositions.** Properties like fragility or solubility, tendencies to manifest in certain conditions, which some hold to be irreducibly basic.

**64. Laws of Nature.** The regularities or necessities governing events; Humeans see them as patterns, while others see real necessitation.

**65. Humean Supervenience.** Lewis's doctrine that all facts supervene on the spatiotemporal arrangement of local qualities, with no necessary connections.

**66. Counterfactuals.** Conditionals about what would have happened had things been otherwise, analysed through possible worlds by Stalnaker and Lewis.

**67. Truthmakers.** The things in the world in virtue of which true statements are true; the question is whether every truth needs one.

**68. Abstract Objects.** Entities like numbers, sets and propositions that are not located in space and time and have no causal power.

**69. Platonism.** The view that abstract objects, especially mathematical ones, exist independently of minds and language.

**70. Fictionalism.** The view that statements about some domain, such as numbers or morals, are useful fictions rather than literal truths.

**71. Events.** Occurrences like a collision or a birth; the debate is whether they are basic particulars or properties of objects at times.

**72. Facts.** What makes true statements true, or true propositions themselves; their status is contested between Russell, Wittgenstein and Strawson.

**73. States of Affairs.** Complexes of an object having a property or objects standing in a relation, taken by Armstrong as the world's basic constituents.

**74. Nothingness.** The idea of absence or non-being; Parmenides banned it, Sartre made it constitutive of consciousness, and Leibniz asked why there is something rather than nothing.

**75. Why Is There Something Rather Than Nothing.** Leibniz's ultimate question of existence, asking why there is a world at all and whether the question has any answer.

**76. Metaontology.** The study of what ontological questions mean, and whether they have objective answers or only answers relative to a framework.

**77. Ontological Commitment.** Quine's idea that a theory is committed to whatever must exist for it to be true, read off from its quantifiers.

**78. Ontological Relativity.** Quine's thesis that what a theory refers to can be fixed only relative to a background language and a mapping to another.

**79. Categories.** The most general kinds of being; Aristotle listed ten, including substance, quantity, quality and relation, and Kant gave twelve concepts of understanding.

**80. Transcendentals.** In scholastic thought, properties of being as such, usually unity, truth and goodness, convertible with being.

**81. Analogy of Being.** The scholastic doctrine that "being" applies to God and creatures neither univocally nor equivocally but by analogy.

**82. Esse and Essentia.** Aquinas's distinction between a thing's act of existing and its essence, which in God alone are identical.

**83. Process Philosophy.** The view, from Whitehead, that events and becoming rather than enduring substances are the basic realities.

**84. Monad.** Leibniz's simple, windowless, soul-like substances of which all reality is composed, each mirroring the universe.

**85. Pre-established Harmony.** Leibniz's doctrine that substances do not interact but were created by God to unfold in perfect agreement.

**86. Occasionalism.** The view that God is the only true cause, and created things merely provide occasions for his action.

**87. Neutral Monism.** The view that mind and matter are both made of one neutral stuff, as held by Russell and Mach.

**88. Conatus.** Spinoza's term for the striving of every thing to persevere in its own being, the root of his psychology and ethics.

**89. Accident.** A property a thing has but could lack without ceasing to be what it is, in contrast to its essence.

**90. Mode.** In Spinoza, a particular way a substance is affected; in scholastic thought, a determination of a thing that is neither substance nor accident.

**91. Appearance and Reality.** The distinction between how things seem and how they are, running from Parmenides and Plato through Bradley.

**92. Immanence and Transcendence.** Whether the divine or ultimate lies within the world or beyond it, a division organising pantheism, theism and much modern thought.

**93. Coherentism about Reality.** The idealist view, as in Bradley, that reality is a single coherent whole and that separate things are abstractions from it.

**94. Internal and External Relations.** Whether relations are grounded in the natures of the things related, as monists hold, or can be external to them, as Russell held.

**95. Existence Precedes Essence.** Sartre's claim that a human being first exists and then defines itself by choice, with no fixed nature given in advance.


## 2. Epistemology

**96. Knowledge.** Traditionally justified true belief; the field asks what knowing requires and how it differs from mere opinion or luck.

**97. Justified True Belief.** The classical analysis of knowledge from Plato's Theaetetus, attacked by Gettier as insufficient.

**98. Gettier Problem.** Edmund Gettier's 1963 counterexamples showing that justified true belief can arise by luck without being knowledge.

**99. Truth.** The property of statements or beliefs that say how things are; correspondence, coherence and pragmatist accounts compete.

**100. Correspondence Theory of Truth.** Truth consists in agreement with the facts; a belief is true when the world is as it says.

**101. Coherence Theory of Truth.** Truth consists in coherence within a comprehensive system of beliefs, favoured by idealists such as Bradley.

**102. Pragmatic Theory of Truth.** Truth is what works or is warranted in the long run of inquiry, as in Peirce, James and Dewey.

**103. Deflationism about Truth.** The view that calling something true adds nothing beyond asserting it, so truth is not a substantive property.

**104. Belief.** The attitude of taking something to be the case; epistemology asks when it is rational and whether it is voluntary.

**105. Justification.** The standing a belief has when it is properly supported; internalists tie it to what the believer can access, externalists to reliable connection.

**106. Foundationalism.** The view that knowledge rests on basic beliefs that need no further support, with all other justified belief built upon them.

**107. Coherentism.** The view that beliefs are justified by fitting together in a mutually supporting web, with no privileged foundation.

**108. Infinitism.** The view that justification rests on an endless chain of reasons, so that no belief is basic.

**109. Regress Problem.** The worry that every justification needs a further justification, leading to infinite regress, circularity or arbitrary stopping.

**110. Internalism and Externalism.** The dispute over whether justification depends only on factors accessible to the believer or can depend on facts outside awareness.

**111. Reliabilism.** The view that a belief is justified or known if produced by a process that reliably yields true beliefs.

**112. Virtue Epistemology.** The view that knowledge is belief that is true because of the believer's intellectual virtues or competences, as developed by Sosa and Zagzebski.

**113. Skepticism.** The doubt that we have knowledge or justified belief in some domain, a challenge answered rather than ignored by most epistemology.

**114. Pyrrhonian Skepticism.** The ancient practice of suspending judgment in the face of equally balanced arguments in order to reach tranquillity.

**115. Academic Skepticism.** The Hellenistic Academy's view that nothing can be known with certainty, though some beliefs are more probable than others.

**116. Cartesian Doubt.** Descartes' method of withholding assent from whatever can be doubted to find an indubitable foundation, ending in the cogito.

**117. Cogito.** Descartes' "I think, therefore I am," the claim that the very act of doubting guarantees the thinker's existence.

**118. Evil Demon Argument.** Descartes' hypothesis of a deceiver who makes all experience misleading, used to test how much can be known.

**119. Brain in a Vat.** Putnam's modern version of the demon scenario, in which a disembodied brain is fed simulated experience.

**120. Closure Principle.** If one knows p and knows that p entails q, one knows q; skeptical arguments exploit it and some answers deny it.

**121. Contextualism.** The view that the standards for "knows" shift with the context of the speaker, so the same claim may be true in one setting and false in another.

**122. Fallibilism.** The view that one can know something even though one's evidence does not rule out error.

**123. Certainty.** A state of complete assurance, which Descartes sought and many later philosophers held to be unattainable for empirical beliefs.

**124. A Priori and A Posteriori.** Knowledge independent of experience versus knowledge that rests on it, a distinction made central by Kant.

**125. Analytic and Synthetic.** Kant's distinction between judgments true by meaning alone and judgments that add information, challenged by Quine.

**126. Synthetic A Priori.** Kant's category of judgments that extend knowledge yet are known independently of experience, such as the claims of mathematics and geometry.

**127. Rationalism.** The view that reason, not sense experience, is the chief source of knowledge, held by Descartes, Spinoza and Leibniz.

**128. Empiricism.** The view that all knowledge derives from experience, held by Locke, Berkeley and Hume.

**129. Innate Ideas.** Concepts or knowledge present in the mind from birth, affirmed by rationalists and denied by Locke.

**130. Tabula Rasa.** Locke's image of the mind as a blank slate on which experience writes.

**131. Sense Data.** The immediate objects of perception, supposed by some philosophers to be what we are directly aware of, rather than physical things.

**132. Perception.** How the mind comes to be aware of the world through the senses, with direct and indirect theories contesting what is perceived.

**133. Direct Realism.** The view that we perceive external objects directly, with nothing mental standing between us and them.

**134. Representative Realism.** The view that we perceive the world through mental representations that stand in for external objects, as in Locke.

**135. Phenomenalism.** The view that physical objects are constructions out of actual and possible sense experiences, advanced by Mill and Ayer.

**136. Problem of Induction.** Hume's challenge that no non-circular argument justifies inferring the unobserved from the observed.

**137. Induction.** Reasoning from observed cases to general conclusions, the engine of empirical science and the target of Hume's doubt.

**138. Abduction.** Inference to the best explanation, introduced by Peirce as reasoning from effects to the hypothesis that would best account for them.

**139. Bayesian Epistemology.** The view that rational belief comes in degrees obeying the probability calculus and is updated by conditionalisation on evidence.

**140. Evidentialism.** The view that one should believe a proposition only to the degree one's evidence supports it, as Clifford and Conee and Feldman maintain.

**141. Pragmatic Encroachment.** The thesis that practical stakes affect whether a belief counts as knowledge.

**142. Testimony.** Knowledge gained by being told; the question is whether trust in others is basic or must be justified by independent checks.

**143. Memory.** The retention of past information; epistemologists ask how it justifies belief and what it means to remember rather than reconstruct.

**144. Epistemic Peer Disagreement.** The problem of what to believe when an equally informed and capable thinker disagrees with you.

**145. Social Epistemology.** The study of knowledge as a social phenomenon, including testimony, expertise, institutions and the spread of belief.

**146. Epistemic Injustice.** Miranda Fricker's term for wrongs done to people as knowers, through testimonial or hermeneutical exclusion.

**147. Standpoint Epistemology.** The view that social position shapes what one can know, and that marginalised standpoints can yield privileged insight into power.

**148. Feminist Epistemology.** A family of views that examine how gender shapes the production and evaluation of knowledge.

**149. Naturalized Epistemology.** Quine's proposal to treat knowledge as a subject for empirical science, replacing normative theory with psychology.

**150. Epistemic Luck.** The way knowledge can be undermined when a belief's truth is due to chance rather than to the believer's competence.

**151. Epistemic Norms.** The standards, such as truth, evidence and coherence, that govern what one ought to believe.

**152. Epistemic Virtue.** Traits like open-mindedness, intellectual courage and carefulness that make a person a good inquirer.

**153. Doxastic Voluntarism.** The question of whether we can choose what to believe, which bears on whether belief can be obligatory.

**154. Pascal's Wager.** Pascal's argument that belief in God is the rational bet given infinite possible reward, which raises the issue of believing on practical grounds.

**155. The Will to Believe.** James's defence of holding a belief without sufficient evidence in genuine, live and momentous options.

**156. Noumenon and Phenomenon.** Kant's contrast between things as they appear and things as they are in themselves, the latter being unknowable.

**157. Thing-in-Itself.** Kant's term for reality as it is independent of our forms of perception, which we can think but never know.

**158. Transcendental Idealism.** Kant's doctrine that space, time and the categories are contributions of the mind, so we know only appearances.

**159. Transcendental Argument.** An argument that identifies necessary conditions of experience or thought, used by Kant and later against skepticism.

**160. The Given.** The supposed immediate, non-conceptual element of experience that grounds knowledge, rejected by Sellars as a myth.

**161. Myth of the Given.** Sellars's attack on the idea that experience provides non-inferential knowledge independent of any conceptual framework.

**162. Two Dogmas of Empiricism.** Quine's critique of the analytic-synthetic distinction and of reductionism, supporting a holistic view of confirmation.

**163. Confirmation Holism.** The Duhem-Quine thesis that hypotheses face the tribunal of experience only together with auxiliary assumptions.

**164. Underdetermination.** The thesis that evidence can fit more than one theory, so data alone cannot decide which is true.

**165. Hinge Epistemology.** Wittgenstein's idea in On Certainty that some propositions are exempt from doubt because they form the framework of inquiry.

**166. Moorean Response.** G. E. Moore's reply to skepticism that he knows he has hands, so that skeptical conclusions are less certain than ordinary knowledge.

**167. Knowledge How and Knowledge That.** Ryle's distinction between practical skill and propositional knowledge, with the dispute over whether the former reduces to the latter.

**168. Self-Knowledge.** Knowledge of one's own mental states, widely thought to be special in being direct and non-observational.

**169. Privileged Access.** The view that one has an authoritative, immediate access to one's own mental states that others lack.

**170. Introspection.** Looking inward at one's own mental states, whose reliability has been challenged by psychology.

**171. Intuition.** A seeming that something is true without explicit inference, treated as evidence in philosophical method.

**172. Thought Experiment.** A scenario devised to test a theory by what we would judge about it, as in Gettier cases and trolley problems.

**173. Experimental Philosophy.** The use of surveys and empirical methods to study the intuitions behind philosophical positions.

**174. Conceptual Analysis.** The attempt to clarify a concept by stating necessary and sufficient conditions for its application.

**175. Reflective Equilibrium.** Rawls's method of adjusting principles and particular judgments until they cohere.

**176. Verificationism.** The view of the logical positivists that a statement is meaningful only if it can be verified empirically or is true by definition.

**177. Falsificationism.** Popper's criterion that scientific claims must be capable of being refuted by observation.

**178. Epistemic Relativism.** The view that what counts as knowledge or justified belief varies with a culture, framework or community.

**179. Paradigm Shift.** Kuhn's term for a revolutionary change in the basic framework of a science, raising questions of incommensurability and progress.

**180. Knowledge First.** Williamson's thesis that knowledge is a basic, unanalysable mental state, not a compound of belief plus other factors.

**181. Safety and Sensitivity.** Modal conditions on knowledge: that one would not easily have believed falsely, or would not have believed had p been false.

**182. Defeaters.** Further evidence that undermines the justification a belief previously had.

**183. Basic Beliefs.** Beliefs that are justified without being inferred from other beliefs, like perceptual or memory beliefs, in foundationalist theory.

**184. Reformed Epistemology.** Plantinga's view that belief in God can be properly basic and justified without arguments.

**185. Wisdom.** Practical and theoretical excellence in understanding and judgment, treated by many traditions as the aim of inquiry beyond knowledge.

**186. Understanding.** A grasp of how things hang together, widely held to be more than mere knowledge of facts.

**187. Hermeneutics.** The theory of interpretation, developed by Schleiermacher, Dilthey and Gadamer, treating understanding as always situated and historical.

**188. Nous.** The Greek term for intellect or intuitive reason, which Plato and Aristotle took to grasp first principles directly and which Plotinus placed second in the hierarchy of being.


## 3. Logic and Philosophy of Mathematics

**189. Logic.** The study of correct inference, concerned with what follows from what and what makes an argument valid.

**190. Validity.** A property of arguments whose conclusion cannot be false if the premises are true.

**191. Soundness.** A valid argument with true premises, which therefore guarantees a true conclusion.

**192. Deduction.** Reasoning in which the conclusion follows necessarily from the premises, as opposed to induction or abduction.

**193. Syllogism.** Aristotle's form of deduction with two premises and a conclusion, such as "all men are mortal; Socrates is a man; so Socrates is mortal".

**194. Law of Non-Contradiction.** The principle that a proposition and its negation cannot both be true in the same respect, which Aristotle called the firmest of principles.

**195. Law of Excluded Middle.** The principle that every proposition is either true or false, rejected in intuitionistic logic.

**196. Law of Identity.** The principle that each thing is identical with itself, the third traditional law of thought.

**197. Propositional Logic.** The logic of sentences combined by connectives such as "and", "or", "not" and "if", independent of their internal structure.

**198. Predicate Logic.** First-order logic, which adds quantifiers and predicates and can express general claims about objects.

**199. Quantifiers.** Expressions such as "all" and "some" that state how many things satisfy a condition, formalised by Frege.

**200. Material Implication.** The truth-functional "if... then" of classical logic, true whenever the antecedent is false, which gives rise to its paradoxes.

**201. Paradoxes of Material Implication.** The oddities that a false statement implies anything and a true one is implied by anything, which motivate relevance logic.

**202. Modal Logic.** The logic of necessity and possibility, given its modern form by C. I. Lewis and Kripke's possible-worlds semantics.

**203. Possible-Worlds Semantics.** Kripke's account of modal truth, on which necessity is truth in all accessible worlds.

**204. Intuitionistic Logic.** Brouwer's constructive logic, which rejects excluded middle and requires that existence claims be backed by a construction.

**205. Constructivism.** The view that mathematical objects exist only if they can be constructed, with truth tied to proof.

**206. Relevance Logic.** A family of logics requiring the premises of a valid argument to be relevant to its conclusion.

**207. Paraconsistent Logic.** Logics that tolerate contradictions without collapsing into triviality, so that not everything follows from a contradiction.

**208. Dialetheism.** Graham Priest's view that some contradictions are true, motivated by the semantic paradoxes.

**209. Many-Valued Logic.** Logics, beginning with Łukasiewicz, that allow truth values beyond true and false.

**210. Fuzzy Logic.** A many-valued approach in which truth comes in degrees, applied to vagueness and to engineering.

**211. Free Logic.** Logic that allows terms that do not refer, avoiding commitments to the existence of what names denote.

**212. Second-Order Logic.** Logic that quantifies over properties and relations as well as individuals, more expressive but lacking a complete proof system.

**213. Completeness Theorem.** Gödel's 1929 result that every valid sentence of first-order logic can be proved.

**214. Incompleteness Theorems.** Gödel's 1931 results that any consistent, sufficiently strong formal system contains true statements it cannot prove and cannot prove its own consistency.

**215. Decidability.** Whether there is an algorithm that settles for every input whether a statement is a theorem; Church and Turing showed first-order logic is undecidable.

**216. Halting Problem.** Turing's proof that no general algorithm can decide whether an arbitrary program halts.

**217. Church-Turing Thesis.** The claim that whatever is effectively computable is computable by a Turing machine.

**218. Computability.** The study of which functions can be calculated by mechanical procedures, central to logic and the philosophy of mind.

**219. Turing Machine.** Turing's abstract model of computation, a tape and a head following simple rules, which defines what is computable.

**220. Proof Theory.** The study of formal proofs and what they establish, associated with Hilbert and Gentzen.

**221. Model Theory.** The study of the relation between formal languages and the structures that satisfy them.

**222. Tarski's Semantic Conception of Truth.** Tarski's definition of truth for formal languages, with the schema that "snow is white" is true if and only if snow is white.

**223. Liar Paradox.** The sentence "this sentence is false", which is true only if false, and which drives research into truth and self-reference.

**224. Russell's Paradox.** Russell's 1901 discovery that the set of all sets not members of themselves leads to contradiction, undermining naive set theory.

**225. Sorites Paradox.** The paradox of the heap: if removing one grain never turns a heap into a non-heap, then a single grain is a heap.

**226. Epistemicism.** The view that vague predicates have sharp boundaries that we cannot know, defended by Williamson.

**227. Supervaluationism.** An approach to vagueness on which a sentence is true if true on every acceptable way of sharpening its terms.

**228. Paradoxes of Zeno.** Arguments, including Achilles and the Tortoise, that motion and plurality are impossible, which prompted work on infinity and limits.

**229. Infinity.** The notion of the unbounded; philosophers distinguish potential from actual infinity, and Cantor made the latter mathematically respectable.

**230. Actual and Potential Infinity.** Aristotle's distinction between a process that can go on forever and a completed infinite totality.

**231. Set Theory.** The mathematical theory of collections, which Cantor founded and which serves as a foundation for much of mathematics.

**232. Cantor's Theorem.** The proof that no set can be matched one-to-one with its power set, which yields different sizes of infinity.

**233. Continuum Hypothesis.** Cantor's conjecture that there is no infinity between the integers and the reals, shown independent of standard set theory.

**234. Axiom of Choice.** The principle that one can pick an element from each of a collection of non-empty sets, controversial for its non-constructive consequences.

**235. Logicism.** The view of Frege and Russell that mathematics is reducible to logic.

**236. Formalism.** Hilbert's view that mathematics is the manipulation of symbols according to rules, with its meaning found in consistency.

**237. Hilbert's Program.** Hilbert's project of founding mathematics on finitary consistency proofs, undermined by Gödel's second theorem.

**238. Intuitionism.** Brouwer's philosophy of mathematics as mental construction, with truth requiring proof rather than correspondence.

**239. Mathematical Platonism.** The view that mathematical objects are real, abstract and mind-independent, and that we discover rather than invent mathematics.

**240. Structuralism in Mathematics.** The view that mathematics studies structures and positions within them rather than objects with intrinsic natures.

**241. Benacerraf's Dilemma.** The tension between a plausible semantics for mathematics, which requires abstract objects, and a plausible epistemology, which requires causal contact.

**242. Indispensability Argument.** Quine and Putnam's argument that we should believe in mathematical objects because science cannot do without them.

**243. Number.** The nature of numbers, treated by Frege as logical objects, by Dedekind as structures, and by Kant as given in intuition.

**244. Frege's Puzzle.** The problem of how "a = b" can be informative though a and b name the same thing, which led Frege to the distinction of sense and reference.

**245. Peano Axioms.** A short set of axioms for the natural numbers, including induction, that characterise arithmetic.

**246. Mathematical Induction.** A proof technique showing that a property holds for all natural numbers if it holds for zero and passes from each number to the next.

**247. Axioms.** Basic statements taken as starting points of a theory, whether as self-evident truths or as stipulations.

**248. Geometry and Non-Euclidean Geometry.** The discovery of consistent geometries denying the parallel postulate, which forced a rethinking of whether space is Euclidean.

**249. Probability.** The study of chance and degrees of belief, whose interpretations include frequency, propensity and subjective credence.

**250. Subjective Probability.** The view that probabilities are degrees of belief constrained only by coherence, defended by Ramsey and de Finetti.

**251. Dutch Book Argument.** The argument that credences violating probability axioms make an agent open to a sure loss in betting.

**252. Decision Theory.** The theory of rational choice under uncertainty, centred on maximising expected utility.

**253. Newcomb's Problem.** A decision puzzle that pits expected-utility reasoning against dominance reasoning when a predictor knows your choice.

**254. Game Theory.** The mathematical study of strategic interaction, which philosophers apply to ethics, convention and social contracts.

**255. Prisoner's Dilemma.** A game in which individually rational choices produce a collectively worse outcome, used to model cooperation and the state of nature.

**256. Social Choice Theory.** The study of how to aggregate individual preferences into collective decisions, with Arrow's theorem showing limits on fair procedures.

**257. Arrow's Impossibility Theorem.** The result that no ranked voting system with several options can satisfy a set of basic fairness conditions.

**258. Fallacy.** A common error in reasoning, such as the ad hominem, the straw man or begging the question.

**259. Begging the Question.** The fallacy of assuming in the premises what the argument claims to prove.

**260. Ad Hominem.** The fallacy of attacking a person rather than the argument that person offers.

**261. Reductio ad Absurdum.** A form of argument that refutes a claim by deriving a contradiction or absurdity from it.

**262. Dialectic.** A method of argument by question, answer and contradiction; Socrates, Hegel and Marx each gave it a distinct role.

**263. Hegelian Dialectic.** Hegel's pattern of development in which a thesis generates its opposite and both are overcome in a higher synthesis.

**264. Conditionals.** Sentences of the form "if A then B", whose logic and truth conditions are disputed among material, strict and probabilistic accounts.

**265. Counterfactual Logic.** The logic of "if it had been the case that A, it would have been B", analysed with similarity among possible worlds.

**266. Deontic Logic.** The logic of obligation and permission, which formalises what ought to be the case.

**267. Epistemic Logic.** The logic of knowledge and belief, which formalises principles such as the closure of knowledge.

**268. Temporal Logic.** The logic of reasoning about time, developed by Arthur Prior with operators for past and future.

**269. Logical Consequence.** The relation between premises and what follows from them, understood by Tarski in terms of truth preservation across interpretations.

**270. Logical Form.** The underlying structure of a sentence revealed by formalisation, central to Russell's and Frege's approaches.

**271. Logical Pluralism.** The view that more than one logic can be correct, depending on the purposes or notion of consequence at stake.

**272. Logical Positivism.** The Vienna Circle's programme of using logic and empirical verification to dissolve metaphysical questions.

**273. Principia Mathematica.** Whitehead and Russell's attempt to derive mathematics from logic using a theory of types.

**274. Theory of Types.** Russell's hierarchy of entities designed to block paradoxes by forbidding self-referential sets.

**275. Category Theory.** A branch of mathematics that studies structures and the mappings between them, proposed by some as an alternative foundation to set theory.


## 4. Philosophy of Language

**276. Meaning.** What words and sentences convey; theories tie it to reference, ideas, use, truth conditions or verification.

**277. Sense and Reference.** Frege's distinction between the object a term picks out and the mode of presentation by which it does so.

**278. Reference.** The relation by which a word or phrase picks out an object, central to theories of names and descriptions.

**279. Proper Names.** Terms like "Aristotle" that pick out individuals; descriptivists and causal theorists disagree about how they refer.

**280. Descriptivism.** The view of Frege and Russell that a name refers by means of a description or cluster of descriptions the speaker associates with it.

**281. Causal Theory of Reference.** Kripke's view that a name refers by a chain of communication leading back to an initial baptism, not by descriptions.

**282. Rigid Designation.** Kripke's term for an expression that refers to the same object in every possible world where it exists.

**283. Theory of Descriptions.** Russell's analysis of phrases like "the present king of France" as quantified claims rather than names, resolving puzzles about empty terms.

**284. Naming and Necessity.** Kripke's 1972 lectures arguing that names are rigid, that identity statements can be necessary a posteriori, and that essences exist.

**285. Necessary A Posteriori.** Truths like "water is H2O" that are necessary yet knowable only through experience.

**286. Contingent A Priori.** Truths such as "the standard metre is one metre long", knowable without experience yet not necessary, as Kripke argued.

**287. Two-Dimensionalism.** A framework that assigns expressions both a primary intension, tied to how we pick things out, and a secondary one, tied to what they pick out in worlds.

**288. Direct Reference Theory.** The view that the meaning of a name or indexical is just its referent, defended by Kaplan and Salmon.

**289. Indexicals.** Words like "I", "here" and "now" whose reference depends on context, analysed by Kaplan as having a stable character.

**290. Character and Content.** Kaplan's distinction between the rule that fixes reference in context and the proposition expressed in a particular context.

**291. Propositions.** The objects of belief and the bearers of truth, thought of as what sentences express across languages.

**292. Truth-Conditional Semantics.** The view, from Davidson and Tarski, that to give a sentence's meaning is to state the conditions under which it is true.

**293. Compositionality.** The principle that the meaning of a complex expression is determined by the meanings of its parts and how they are combined.

**294. Context Principle.** Frege's maxim that words have meaning only in the context of a sentence.

**295. Ideational Theory of Meaning.** Locke's view that words signify the ideas in the speaker's mind.

**296. Use Theory of Meaning.** Wittgenstein's thesis that the meaning of a word is its use in the language.

**297. Language Games.** Wittgenstein's term for the activities in which words are embedded, stressing that meaning is rooted in shared practice.

**298. Private Language Argument.** Wittgenstein's argument that a language whose words refer to a speaker's inner sensations alone, and that no one else can understand, is impossible.

**299. Rule-Following.** Wittgenstein's puzzle about what makes a use of a word correct, which Kripke read as a skeptical paradox about meaning.

**300. Kripkenstein.** Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein's rule-following considerations, claiming that no fact about me fixes what I mean by "plus".

**301. Family Resemblance.** Wittgenstein's idea that concepts like "game" are unified by overlapping similarities, not by a common essence.

**302. Picture Theory of Meaning.** The Tractatus view that a proposition is a picture of a possible state of affairs, sharing its logical form with reality.

**303. Said and Shown.** The Tractarian distinction between what can be stated and what can only be shown by the form of language.

**304. Ordinary Language Philosophy.** The Oxford approach of Austin and Ryle that philosophical problems arise from, and can be dissolved by attention to, everyday usage.

**305. Speech Acts.** Austin's and Searle's idea that to say something is to do something, such as promising, warning or asserting.

**306. Performative Utterance.** A statement like "I promise" that performs an action by being uttered, rather than describing it.

**307. Illocutionary Force.** The kind of act, such as asking, ordering or asserting, performed in saying something.

**308. Implicature.** Grice's term for what a speaker suggests without saying, governed by shared conversational maxims.

**309. Cooperative Principle.** Grice's idea that conversation is governed by norms of quantity, quality, relation and manner.

**310. Pragmatics.** The study of how context contributes to meaning beyond the literal content of sentences.

**311. Presupposition.** What a statement takes for granted, such as the existence of a king in "the king is bald", and whose failure leaves it neither true nor false, on Strawson's view.

**312. Semantic Externalism.** The thesis, from Putnam and Burge, that meaning and content depend partly on the environment and not just on the head.

**313. Twin Earth.** Putnam's thought experiment in which an identical-seeming liquid with a different chemistry shows that "meaning ain't in the head".

**314. Radical Translation.** Quine's thought experiment about interpreting a wholly unknown language, used to argue that meaning is indeterminate.

**315. Indeterminacy of Translation.** Quine's thesis that many incompatible translation manuals fit all the behavioural evidence equally well.

**316. Gavagai.** Quine's example of a native word used when a rabbit is seen, which cannot be pinned to "rabbit" rather than "undetached rabbit part".

**317. Principle of Charity.** Davidson's and Quine's maxim that interpretation should maximise the truth and rationality of the speaker's beliefs.

**318. Radical Interpretation.** Davidson's extension of Quine's idea, in which a theory of truth for a speaker's language is built from evidence of what they hold true.

**319. Natural Language and Formal Language.** The contrast between languages that grew up spontaneously and artificial languages with explicit syntax and semantics, as in the ideal language tradition.

**320. Linguistic Turn.** The twentieth-century shift in philosophy toward analysing language as the means of solving or dissolving philosophical problems.

**321. Sapir-Whorf Hypothesis.** The thesis that the language one speaks shapes or limits one's thought, in strong and weak versions.

**322. Universal Grammar.** Chomsky's proposal of an innate faculty that underlies the grammars of all human languages.

**323. Poverty of the Stimulus.** Chomsky's argument that children learn language from too little evidence to do so without innate structure.

**324. Innateness of Language.** The thesis that humans are born with specifically linguistic knowledge, central to the rationalist side of cognitive science.

**325. Syntax and Semantics.** The distinction between the formal rules that govern sentence structure and the meanings those structures carry.

**326. Metaphor.** Figurative speech in which one thing is described in terms of another, raising the question of whether it has a distinctive meaning.

**327. Ambiguity.** The presence of more than one meaning in an expression, which logical analysis seeks to disentangle.

**328. Analytic Philosophy.** The tradition, from Frege, Russell and Moore, that prizes clarity, logical analysis and close argument.

**329. Emotivism.** The view of Ayer and Stevenson that moral statements express attitudes and do not state facts.

**330. Expressivism.** The wider view that certain statements, notably moral ones, express mental states other than belief.

**331. Prescriptivism.** R. M. Hare's view that moral judgments are universalisable commands telling us what to do.

**332. Speech and Writing.** Derrida's critique of the tradition's privileging of speech as present meaning over writing as a derivative sign.

**333. Différance.** Derrida's coinage for the play of difference and deferral that makes meaning possible and never fully stable.

**334. Deconstruction.** Derrida's mode of reading that exposes the tensions and hierarchies on which texts depend, so that their meaning is unstable.

**335. Structuralism.** The approach, from Saussure and Lévi-Strauss, of explaining phenomena by their position in systems of relations.

**336. Signifier and Signified.** Saussure's two sides of the linguistic sign: the sound-image and the concept it evokes, linked arbitrarily.

**337. Semiotics.** The general study of signs and their meaning, developed by Peirce and Saussure.

**338. Peirce's Triad of Signs.** Peirce's classification of signs as icons, indices and symbols by their relation to what they stand for.

**339. Discourse.** Structured ways of speaking that shape what can be said and known, a central concept for Foucault.

**340. Hermeneutic Circle.** The idea that interpreting a whole requires understanding its parts, and understanding the parts requires some grasp of the whole.


## 5. Philosophy of Mind

**341. Mind-Body Problem.** The question of how mental states relate to the physical body and brain, central to philosophy since Descartes.

**342. Substance Dualism.** Descartes' view that mind and body are two distinct kinds of substance, one thinking and unextended, the other extended and unthinking.

**343. Property Dualism.** The view that there is one kind of substance, but mental properties are not reducible to physical ones.

**344. Interaction Problem.** The difficulty of explaining how an immaterial mind could causally affect a material body, pressed by Princess Elisabeth against Descartes.

**345. Epiphenomenalism.** The view that mental states are caused by physical processes but have no causal effect on them.

**346. Physicalism.** The view that everything, including mental states, is physical or depends on the physical.

**347. Identity Theory.** The view of Place and Smart that mental states are identical with brain states.

**348. Multiple Realizability.** The idea, due to Putnam, that the same mental state can be realised in different physical systems, an objection to type identity.

**349. Functionalism.** The view that mental states are defined by their causal roles in relation to inputs, outputs and other states, not by their material makeup.

**350. Behaviourism.** The view that mental states are dispositions to behave, and that psychology should concern only observable behaviour.

**351. Eliminative Materialism.** The view of the Churchlands that folk psychology is a false theory and that beliefs and desires will be replaced by neuroscience.

**352. Folk Psychology.** Our everyday framework of explaining behaviour by beliefs and desires, whose scientific standing is disputed.

**353. Anomalous Monism.** Davidson's view that each mental event is a physical event but that there are no strict psychophysical laws.

**354. Consciousness.** The having of subjective experience, the central puzzle of the philosophy of mind.

**355. Hard Problem of Consciousness.** Chalmers's question of why physical processes give rise to subjective experience at all.

**356. Qualia.** The intrinsic, subjectively experienced qualities of mental states, such as the redness of red.

**357. What Is It Like to Be a Bat.** Nagel's argument that consciousness has an irreducibly subjective character that objective science cannot capture.

**358. Knowledge Argument.** Jackson's story of Mary, a colour scientist who learns something new on seeing red for the first time, thought to refute physicalism.

**359. Explanatory Gap.** Levine's term for the gap between physical explanations of the brain and an explanation of why there is experience.

**360. Philosophical Zombie.** Chalmers's imagined being physically identical to us but lacking consciousness, used to argue against physicalism.

**361. Inverted Spectrum.** The thought experiment of a person whose colour experiences are systematically swapped with ours without any behavioural difference.

**362. Panpsychism.** The view that mind or experience is a fundamental feature of all matter.

**363. Mysterianism.** McGinn's view that the mind-body problem is real but beyond the cognitive reach of human beings.

**364. Higher-Order Theories.** Theories on which a mental state is conscious when one has a higher-order thought or perception of it.

**365. Global Workspace Theory.** Baars's view that consciousness is the broadcasting of information to many cognitive systems at once.

**366. Integrated Information Theory.** Tononi's view that consciousness corresponds to a system's integrated information, measured by phi.

**367. Access and Phenomenal Consciousness.** Block's distinction between information available for reasoning and action, and the raw experience itself.

**368. Intentionality.** The "aboutness" of mental states, their directedness at objects or states of affairs, which Brentano called the mark of the mental.

**369. Brentano's Thesis.** Brentano's claim that intentionality distinguishes the mental from the physical.

**370. Mental Representation.** The idea that thought involves internal states that stand for things beyond themselves.

**371. Language of Thought.** Fodor's hypothesis that thinking takes place in an inner symbolic system with syntax and compositional semantics.

**372. Computational Theory of Mind.** The view that mental processes are computations over mental representations.

**373. Chinese Room.** Searle's thought experiment showing that symbol manipulation alone does not suffice for understanding.

**374. Strong and Weak AI.** Searle's distinction between the claim that a suitably programmed computer has a mind and the claim that it merely models one.

**375. Turing Test.** Turing's proposal that a machine that is indistinguishable from a human in conversation should count as thinking.

**376. Artificial Intelligence.** The project of building machines that perform tasks requiring intelligence, which poses questions of understanding and moral status.

**377. Connectionism.** The approach that models cognition with networks of simple interconnected units rather than explicit symbolic rules.

**378. Frame Problem.** The difficulty of specifying which things change and which stay the same when an agent acts, a challenge for artificial intelligence.

**379. Extended Mind.** Clark and Chalmers's thesis that cognitive processes can extend into tools and the environment, such as a notebook serving as memory.

**380. Embodied Cognition.** The view that cognition depends on the body and its interactions with the world, not just on the brain.

**381. Enactivism.** The view that minds arise from the active engagement of organisms with their environments.

**382. 4E Cognition.** The umbrella view that cognition is embodied, embedded, enacted and extended.

**383. Predictive Processing.** The theory that the brain continually generates predictions about its inputs and learns by reducing prediction error.

**384. Mental Causation.** The problem of how mental states can cause behaviour if the physical realm is causally closed.

**385. Causal Closure of the Physical.** The principle that every physical event with a cause has a sufficient physical cause.

**386. Exclusion Argument.** Kim's argument that mental causation is threatened by sufficient physical causes, leaving no work for the mental.

**387. Mental Content.** What a thought is about or says; narrow content is fixed by the head, broad content also by the environment.

**388. Propositional Attitudes.** Mental states, such as belief and desire, that take a proposition as their object.

**389. Belief-Desire Psychology.** The model on which actions are explained by what an agent wants and believes.

**390. Emotions.** Affective states such as fear and anger; accounts treat them as feelings, judgments, or embodied appraisals.

**391. Moods.** Diffuse affective states without a specific object, such as anxiety or elation, studied in phenomenology.

**392. Imagination.** The capacity to represent what is not present, important to thought, fiction and modal knowledge.

**393. Perception and Illusion.** The study of how we see the world and how experience can mislead, raising questions about the nature of appearance.

**394. Disjunctivism.** The view that veridical perception and hallucination are fundamentally different kinds of state, not a common experience.

**395. Representationalism.** The view that the phenomenal character of experience is exhausted by what it represents.

**396. Attention.** The selective focus of mind on certain items, studied for its role in consciousness and perception.

**397. Unity of Consciousness.** The fact that experiences are experienced together as belonging to a single subject.

**398. Binding Problem.** The puzzle of how features processed in different brain areas are combined into a single unified experience.

**399. Split Brain.** Cases, after severing of the corpus callosum, that suggest a single person may have divided consciousness.

**400. Self.** The subject of experience; theories range from an enduring soul to a bundle of perceptions to a narrative construction.

**401. Bundle Theory of the Self.** Hume's claim that the self is nothing but a bundle of perceptions in perpetual flux.

**402. No-Self.** The Buddhist and Humean view that there is no unchanging self underlying experience.

**403. Narrative Self.** The idea that selfhood is constituted by the stories we tell about our lives.

**404. Minimal Self.** The pre-reflective sense of being the subject of experience, defended by phenomenologists and Gallagher and Zahavi.

**405. Other Minds.** The problem of how we can know that others have minds, given that we perceive only their behaviour.

**406. Theory of Mind.** The capacity to attribute mental states to others, studied through theory-theory and simulation accounts.

**407. Simulation Theory.** The view that we understand others by simulating their mental states in ourselves.

**408. Animal Minds.** Questions of whether and how animals think and feel, with consequences for ethics.


## 6. Ethics

**409. The Good.** What is worth pursuing or valuing; Plato placed the Form of the Good at the summit of reality and ethics asks what makes things good.

**410. Virtue.** A stable trait of character that enables excellent action, central to Aristotle's ethics.

**411. Virtue Ethics.** The approach that makes character, not rules or consequences, the basis of morality.

**412. Eudaimonia.** Aristotle's term for human flourishing, the final end of life, achieved through virtuous activity.

**413. Doctrine of the Mean.** Aristotle's view that each virtue lies between an excess and a deficiency, such as courage between rashness and cowardice.

**414. Practical Wisdom.** Aristotle's phronesis, the capacity to see what to do in particular situations, which unifies the virtues.

**415. Cardinal Virtues.** Wisdom, courage, temperance and justice, the four hinge virtues of the classical tradition.

**416. Habituation.** Aristotle's idea that virtues are acquired by practice, becoming second nature.

**417. Akrasia.** Weakness of will, acting against one's better judgment, which Socrates denied was possible.

**418. Socratic Intellectualism.** Socrates' view that virtue is knowledge and that no one does wrong knowingly.

**419. Unity of the Virtues.** The thesis that the virtues are inseparable, so that one cannot truly have one without the others.

**420. Consequentialism.** The view that the rightness of an act depends only on its outcomes.

**421. Utilitarianism.** The view of Bentham and Mill that the right act maximises overall happiness or welfare.

**422. Principle of Utility.** Bentham's principle that actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness and wrong as they tend to produce its reverse.

**423. Hedonism.** The view that pleasure is the only intrinsic good and pain the only intrinsic evil.

**424. Higher and Lower Pleasures.** Mill's distinction holding that intellectual pleasures are intrinsically more valuable than bodily ones.

**425. Act and Rule Utilitarianism.** The contrast between judging each act by its consequences and judging acts by rules whose general adoption would be best.

**426. Preference Utilitarianism.** The view that what matters is the satisfaction of preferences, developed by Hare and Singer.

**427. Welfare and Well-Being.** Theories of what makes a life go well, whether pleasure, desire satisfaction or objective goods.

**428. Experience Machine.** Nozick's thought experiment designed to show that we value more than pleasurable experiences.

**429. Repugnant Conclusion.** Parfit's result that total utilitarianism implies a vast population with lives barely worth living would be better than a smaller flourishing one.

**430. Deontology.** The view that some acts are right or wrong in themselves, regardless of their consequences.

**431. Categorical Imperative.** Kant's supreme principle of morality, commanding that we act only on maxims we could will as universal laws.

**432. Hypothetical Imperative.** A command of reason that tells us what to do given a goal we already have, in contrast to a categorical one.

**433. Universal Law Formula.** The first version of Kant's imperative, which tests maxims by whether they can be universalised without contradiction.

**434. Humanity Formula.** Kant's imperative to treat humanity, in oneself and others, always as an end and never merely as a means.

**435. Good Will.** Kant's claim that the only thing good without qualification is a will that acts from duty.

**436. Duty.** What morality requires of us; Kant held that actions have moral worth only when done from respect for duty.

**437. Autonomy.** Self-governance; for Kant, the will's giving itself the moral law.

**438. Dignity.** The unconditional worth of persons, which Kant opposed to price.

**439. Moral Law.** The principle of morality that binds all rational beings, for Kant given by reason.

**440. Prima Facie Duties.** W. D. Ross's idea of several duties, each binding unless outweighed by another in a given situation.

**441. Rights Theory.** The view that moral standing is expressed in individual rights, which limit what may be done to persons.

**442. Natural Rights.** Rights held to belong to people as such, independent of any government, as in Locke.

**443. Human Rights.** Fundamental claims all persons hold by virtue of their humanity, enshrined in modern declarations.

**444. Contractualism.** Scanlon's view that an act is wrong if it is forbidden by principles no one could reasonably reject.

**445. Social Contract Ethics.** The view that morality consists in rules agreed upon by rational persons for mutual benefit.

**446. Ethics of Care.** The view of Gilligan and Noddings that morality centres on relationships and attentiveness to particular others.

**447. Moral Particularism.** The view that morality has no general principles and that moral reasons depend on the case.

**448. Moral Realism.** The view that there are objective moral facts independent of what anyone thinks.

**449. Anti-Realism in Ethics.** The family of views denying that there are mind-independent moral facts.

**450. Error Theory.** Mackie's view that moral claims aim at objective truth but are all false because no such facts exist.

**451. Moral Relativism.** The view that moral truth or justification is relative to a culture or individual.

**452. Cultural Relativism.** The thesis that moral standards vary with culture and have no independent authority.

**453. Moral Nihilism.** The view that nothing is morally right or wrong, or that moral claims have no truth.

**454. Naturalism in Ethics.** The view that moral properties are natural properties that science can study.

**455. Non-Naturalism.** Moore's view that goodness is a simple, indefinable, non-natural property.

**456. Naturalistic Fallacy.** Moore's charge that it is an error to define "good" in terms of any natural property.

**457. Is-Ought Problem.** Hume's observation that one cannot derive an ought from an is without a further evaluative premise.

**458. Open Question Argument.** Moore's argument that "is it good?" stays open whatever natural property we name, so good is not identical to it.

**459. Moral Sentimentalism.** The view of Hume and Smith that morality is grounded in sentiments such as sympathy.

**460. Sympathy.** In Hume and Smith, the capacity to feel with others, which underlies moral approval.

**461. Impartial Spectator.** Adam Smith's imagined fair observer, whose approval is the standard of conduct.

**462. Moral Intuitionism.** The view that we know basic moral truths directly, without inference.

**463. Constructivism in Ethics.** The view that moral truths are determined by the outcome of an idealised procedure of reasoning.

**464. Divine Command Theory.** The view that morality consists in the commands of God.

**465. Euthyphro Dilemma.** Plato's question whether the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, or pious because it is loved.

**466. Natural Law.** The view, from Aquinas, that moral principles are grounded in human nature and discoverable by reason.

**467. Doctrine of Double Effect.** The principle that a harm may be permissible as a foreseen side effect though impermissible as an intended means.

**468. Doing and Allowing.** The distinction between causing harm and failing to prevent it, which many hold to differ morally.

**469. Trolley Problem.** Foot's and Thomson's dilemmas about diverting a runaway trolley, probing the limits of consequentialism and deontology.

**470. Supererogation.** Acts that are good but beyond the call of duty.

**471. Moral Luck.** Williams's and Nagel's puzzle that we are judged by factors beyond our control.

**472. Moral Responsibility.** The conditions under which a person is rightly praised or blamed for an act.

**473. Moral Psychology.** The study of the mental capacities underlying morality, such as motivation, emotion and character.

**474. Moral Motivation.** The question of what moves us to act morally, with internalists tying judgment to motivation.

**475. Moral Status.** Whether a being matters morally for its own sake, whether infants, animals or artificial beings.

**476. Moral Circle.** The boundary of beings given moral consideration, which Singer argues has been widened over history.

**477. Animal Ethics.** The study of our obligations to animals, including Singer's critique of speciesism and Regan's rights view.

**478. Speciesism.** The charge that favouring one's own species without a morally relevant ground is a prejudice like racism.

**479. Environmental Ethics.** The study of our obligations to nature, including deep ecology and the land ethic.

**480. Deep Ecology.** Naess's view that nature has intrinsic value and that human interests do not override it.

**481. Land Ethic.** Leopold's proposal that we treat the land community as a moral object.

**482. Future Generations.** The question of what we owe to people not yet born, and how to weigh their interests.

**483. Population Ethics.** The study of the value of different possible populations, with puzzles like the repugnant conclusion.

**484. Effective Altruism.** The movement and view that we should use evidence and reason to do as much good as possible.

**485. Duty of Beneficence.** Singer's argument that affluent people must give to relieve suffering when they can do so at little cost.

**486. Drowning Child.** Singer's analogy that failing to aid distant strangers is like refusing to save a child at small cost.

**487. Bioethics.** The ethics of medicine and biology, including consent, life and death, and enhancement.

**488. Autonomy in Medicine.** The principle that competent patients have the right to decide about their own treatment.

**489. Informed Consent.** A person's voluntary agreement to a procedure on the basis of adequate understanding.

**490. Euthanasia.** Deliberately ending life to relieve suffering, which raises issues of autonomy and the sanctity of life.

**491. Just War Theory.** The tradition of criteria governing when war is justified and how it may be fought.

**492. Punishment.** The infliction of a penalty for wrongdoing, justified by retribution, deterrence or reform.

**493. Retributivism.** The view that wrongdoers deserve punishment in proportion to their wrongdoing.

**494. Deterrence.** The justification of punishment by its effect in discouraging future offences.

**495. Machine Ethics.** The study of how artificial agents should act and what moral limits apply to their design.

**496. Golden Rule.** The principle, found across traditions, to treat others as one would wish to be treated.

**497. Altruism.** Acting for the benefit of others, and the debate whether it exists or reduces to self-interest.

**498. Psychological Egoism.** The claim that all human action is ultimately motivated by self-interest.

**499. Ethical Egoism.** The normative view that each person ought to pursue their own interest.

**500. Master and Slave Morality.** Nietzsche's contrast between a morality of strength and one born of resentment among the weak.

**501. Genealogy of Morals.** Nietzsche's historical critique tracing moral concepts to the interests and psychology that produced them.

**502. Ressentiment.** Nietzsche's term for the festering resentment of the powerless, which he saw as the root of slave morality.

**503. Will to Power.** Nietzsche's idea that the basic drive of living things is to expand and exert their strength.

**504. Reasons for Action.** The considerations that count in favour of acting, central to debates about internalism and externalism.

**505. Internalism about Reasons.** Williams's thesis that a reason must connect to the agent's existing motivations.

**506. Rationality and Morality.** The question of whether it is always rational to act morally, posed by Plato and Sidgwick.

**507. Dualism of Practical Reason.** Sidgwick's worry that reason supports both egoism and impartial benevolence, with no way to reconcile them.

**508. Value Pluralism.** Berlin's view that human values are many and sometimes irreconcilable, with no single scale for weighing them.

**509. Intrinsic and Instrumental Value.** The distinction between what is valuable for its own sake and what is valuable as a means.

**510. Fitting Attitude Analysis.** The view that to be valuable is to merit a favourable attitude.

**511. Thick and Thin Concepts.** The contrast between richly descriptive evaluative terms like "cruel" and thin ones like "good".

**512. Dirty Hands.** The problem that political and moral life can require doing wrong in order to do right.

**513. Demandingness Objection.** The complaint that consequentialism asks too much of agents.

**514. Integrity.** Williams's idea that morality must leave room for the projects and commitments that make a life one's own.


## 7. Political, Legal and Social Philosophy

**515. Justice.** Giving each their due; the central concept of political philosophy, variously understood as order (Plato), fairness (Rawls) or entitlement (Nozick).

**516. Social Contract.** The idea that political authority rests on an agreement among free persons, developed by Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau and Rawls.

**517. State of Nature.** The imagined condition before government, which Hobbes saw as a war of all against all and Locke as governed by natural law.

**518. Leviathan.** Hobbes's image of the sovereign state, created by contract so that people may escape the violence of the state of nature.

**519. General Will.** Rousseau's notion of the will of the people directed at the common good, to which each citizen submits and so remains free.

**520. Sovereignty.** Supreme authority within a territory; theorists ask who holds it and what limits it.

**521. Political Obligation.** Whether and why citizens must obey the law of their state.

**522. Consent of the Governed.** The principle that legitimate government rests on the agreement of those it rules.

**523. Legitimacy.** The right of a political authority to rule, as distinct from its mere power.

**524. Authority.** The right to command and to be obeyed, which anarchists such as Wolff deny that any state can have.

**525. Anarchism.** The view that the state is unnecessary and illegitimate and that society can be organised without coercive government.

**526. Liberalism.** The political tradition that prizes individual liberty, equality of rights and limited government.

**527. Classical Liberalism.** The doctrine, from Locke and Smith, of limited government, free markets and individual rights.

**528. Negative and Positive Liberty.** Berlin's contrast between freedom from interference and freedom as self-mastery or the capacity to act.

**529. Harm Principle.** Mill's rule that power may be exercised over a person against their will only to prevent harm to others.

**530. Republicanism.** The tradition that defines freedom as non-domination and stresses civic virtue and self-rule.

**531. Freedom as Non-Domination.** Pettit's view that liberty is the absence of being subject to another's arbitrary will, even if they do not interfere.

**532. Conservatism.** The tradition, from Burke, that values inherited institutions and distrusts rapid, abstract reform.

**533. Communitarianism.** The view of Sandel, MacIntyre and Taylor that the self is formed by communities and that liberalism neglects this.

**534. Libertarianism.** The view that individual liberty and property rights should be maximally protected and the state minimal.

**535. Minimal State.** Nozick's thesis that only a state limited to protection against force, theft and fraud can be justified.

**536. Entitlement Theory.** Nozick's view that a distribution is just if it arose through just acquisition and transfer, whatever its pattern.

**537. Justice as Fairness.** Rawls's theory that the principles of justice are those that free and equal persons would choose in a fair initial situation.

**538. Original Position.** Rawls's hypothetical situation in which people choose principles of justice without knowing their own place in society.

**539. Veil of Ignorance.** The device of the original position that hides one's class, talents and conception of the good so that choice is impartial.

**540. Difference Principle.** Rawls's principle that inequalities are just only if they benefit the least advantaged.

**541. Overlapping Consensus.** Rawls's idea of a political conception of justice endorsed by citizens who hold differing comprehensive doctrines.

**542. Public Reason.** The idea that political decisions should be justified by reasons all reasonable citizens can accept.

**543. Egalitarianism.** The view that equality should be a central aim of social arrangements.

**544. Luck Egalitarianism.** The view that inequalities are unjust when they stem from luck and not from people's choices.

**545. Capabilities Approach.** Sen's and Nussbaum's view that justice concerns what people are actually able to do and be.

**546. Distributive Justice.** The question of how benefits and burdens of social life should be allocated.

**547. Desert.** The idea that people deserve rewards or penalties in virtue of what they have done.

**548. Meritocracy.** A system in which positions and rewards go to those with talent and effort, subject to critique for hidden inequalities.

**549. Socialism.** The family of views that favours collective or social ownership and control of the means of production.

**550. Marxism.** The theory of Marx and Engels that history is driven by class conflict and that capitalism will be succeeded by communism.

**551. Historical Materialism.** Marx's thesis that the economic base shapes the legal, political and ideological superstructure.

**552. Class Struggle.** Marx's thesis that history is the history of conflict between classes defined by their relation to production.

**553. Alienation.** Marx's analysis of how workers under capitalism are estranged from their labour, its product, others and themselves.

**554. Surplus Value.** Marx's term for the unpaid labour appropriated by the capitalist, the source of profit.

**555. Commodity Fetishism.** Marx's idea that social relations among people appear as relations among things.

**556. Ideology.** A system of ideas that obscures and legitimises existing social power, a concept central to Marxist critique.

**557. Hegemony.** Gramsci's term for domination secured through cultural leadership and consent rather than force alone.

**558. Critical Theory.** The Frankfurt School's project of diagnosing and challenging domination through interdisciplinary social critique.

**559. Communicative Action.** Habermas's theory that social coordination rests on reaching mutual understanding through reasoned discourse.

**560. Public Sphere.** Habermas's term for the space where citizens discuss matters of common concern and form public opinion.

**561. Deliberative Democracy.** The view that democratic legitimacy comes from public reasoning among free and equal citizens.

**562. Democracy.** Rule by the people; theorists debate its justification, its forms and its limits, from Athens to the present.

**563. Tyranny of the Majority.** Tocqueville's and Mill's worry that democratic majorities can oppress minorities.

**564. Separation of Powers.** Montesquieu's principle that dividing legislative, executive and judicial authority guards against tyranny.

**565. Rule of Law.** The ideal that all are subject to law that is public, general and impartially applied.

**566. Constitutionalism.** The view that government should be limited by a fundamental law.

**567. Cosmopolitanism.** The view that all human beings belong to a single moral community and that national borders carry limited moral weight.

**568. Global Justice.** The study of what justice requires across borders, concerning poverty, trade and migration.

**569. Nationalism.** The view that the nation is the proper basis of political community, with loyalty and self-determination as key values.

**570. Multiculturalism.** The view that cultural diversity should be recognised and accommodated by public institutions.

**571. Recognition.** Hegel's, Taylor's and Honneth's idea that identity depends on being acknowledged by others and that misrecognition is a harm.

**572. Feminism.** The movement and theory concerned with the equality of women and the critique of gender hierarchy.

**573. Patriarchy.** A social order in which men dominate, subject to analysis in feminist theory.

**574. Intersectionality.** Crenshaw's term for how overlapping systems of oppression, such as race and gender, compound each other.

**575. Gender as Performance.** Butler's thesis that gender is constituted by repeated acts and not a natural fact.

**576. Critical Race Theory.** The analysis of how law and institutions embed racial hierarchy.

**577. Colonialism and Postcolonial Theory.** The critique of imperial domination and its legacies, developed by Fanon, Said and Spivak.

**578. Orientalism.** Said's analysis of how Western representations of the East served the cause of domination.

**579. Biopower.** Foucault's term for forms of power that manage the life of populations.

**580. Panopticon.** Bentham's design for a prison with constant surveillance, which Foucault used as a model of modern disciplinary power.

**581. Power/Knowledge.** Foucault's claim that knowledge and power are intertwined, so that regimes of truth are also regimes of control.

**582. Social Construction.** The thesis that categories such as gender and race are products of social practice, not natural facts.

**583. Civil Disobedience.** Open, non-violent breach of law on grounds of conscience, as defended by Thoreau, Gandhi and King.

**584. Revolution.** A fundamental and rapid overthrow of a political order, whose justification is a perennial subject for political theory.

**585. Legal Positivism.** The view that the existence of law depends on social facts and not on its moral merit.

**586. Natural Law Theory of Law.** The view that an unjust law is not truly law, or that law is bound up with morality.

**587. Hart's Rule of Recognition.** Hart's idea of the social rule that identifies which norms count as valid law in a legal system.

**588. Law as Integrity.** Dworkin's view that judges should interpret law as a coherent expression of moral principle.

**589. Property.** The right to use and exclude others from things; Locke grounded it in labour, others in convention or social need.

**590. Labour Theory of Property.** Locke's view that mixing one's labour with unowned things gives one a right to them.

**591. Tragedy of the Commons.** Hardin's account of how shared resources are depleted when individuals pursue self-interest.

**592. Paternalism.** Interfering with a person's liberty for their own good, a standing problem for liberal theory.

**593. Privacy.** The claim to control access to oneself and information about oneself, challenged by modern technology.

**594. Freedom of Speech.** The right to express opinions, whose limits are debated, as in Mill's defence of free discussion.

**595. Toleration.** The practice of putting up with what one dislikes or disapproves of, defended by Locke and Mill.

**596. Secularism.** The principle of separating religious authority from the state.

**597. Iron Cage.** Weber's metaphor for the way rationalised bureaucratic life constrains and disenchants modern individuals.

**598. Disenchantment.** Weber's term for the loss of magic and ultimate meaning in modernity as rational calculation spreads.

**599. Anomie.** Durkheim's term for a condition in which social norms break down and individuals lose moral guidance.

**600. Collective Representations.** Durkheim's idea that shared beliefs and symbols are social facts that shape individual thought.

**601. Political Realism.** The tradition, from Thucydides and Machiavelli, that stresses power and interest in the conduct of states.

**602. Virtù and Fortuna.** Machiavelli's contrast between the skill of the prince and the force of chance in political success.

**603. End of History.** Fukuyama's thesis, drawing on Hegel and Kojève, that liberal democracy represents the final form of government.

**604. Totalitarianism.** Arendt's term for a novel form of rule that uses terror and ideology to dominate every part of life.

**605. Banality of Evil.** Arendt's phrase for wrongdoing committed by ordinary people who do not think about what they are doing.

**606. Vita Activa.** Arendt's division of the active life into labour, work and action, with action as the highest.


## 8. Aesthetics

**607. Beauty.** The quality of things that please when perceived or contemplated; philosophers disagree whether it is objective or subjective.

**608. Aesthetics.** The philosophical study of beauty, art and aesthetic experience, named by Baumgarten in the eighteenth century.

**609. Aesthetic Experience.** A distinctive kind of attention to an object for its own sake, marked by pleasure and absorption.

**610. Taste.** The capacity to judge beauty; Hume sought a standard of taste in the verdicts of ideal critics.

**611. Standard of Taste.** Hume's proposal that aesthetic value is revealed by the joint judgment of qualified critics.

**612. Judgment of Taste.** Kant's claim that a judgment of beauty is subjective yet claims universal assent.

**613. Disinterestedness.** The idea that aesthetic pleasure is free of desire for the object's use or possession, as in Kant and Schopenhauer.

**614. Purposiveness without Purpose.** Kant's phrase for the formal fit of a beautiful object, which seems designed though it serves no end.

**615. Sublime.** The experience of overwhelming greatness or power, such as a storm or mountain, which Burke and Kant contrasted with beauty.

**616. Genius.** Kant's term for the innate talent through which nature gives the rule to art.

**617. Mimesis.** Imitation or representation; Plato distrusted it and Aristotle saw it as the basis of art.

**618. Catharsis.** Aristotle's term for the purging or clarifying of emotions such as pity and fear through tragedy.

**619. Tragedy.** A dramatic form depicting suffering and downfall, whose appeal Aristotle, Hegel and Nietzsche each explained.

**620. Apollonian and Dionysian.** Nietzsche's contrast between art of order and form and art of ecstasy and dissolution.

**621. Expression Theory.** The view of Tolstoy and Collingwood that art is the expression of emotion.

**622. Formalism in Art.** The view that the value of an artwork lies in its form and not in its content or subject.

**623. Significant Form.** Clive Bell's thesis that what makes something art is its arrangement of lines and colours.

**624. Institutional Theory of Art.** Dickie's and Danto's view that something is art when the artworld confers that status on it.

**625. Artworld.** Danto's term for the theory and institutions that make it possible to see something as art.

**626. Definition of Art.** The question of whether art can be defined, with Weitz denying that it has necessary and sufficient conditions.

**627. Ready-Made.** Duchamp's practice of presenting ordinary objects as art, which forced the question of what makes art.

**628. Authenticity and Forgery.** The question of whether the origin of a work matters to its aesthetic value, when a perfect copy looks identical.

**629. Intentional Fallacy.** Wimsatt and Beardsley's claim that an author's intention is neither available nor desirable as a standard for judging a work.

**630. Death of the Author.** Barthes's thesis that a text's meaning is made by readers and not fixed by its writer.

**631. Interpretation.** The attempt to make sense of artworks and texts, with disputes over the role of authorial intention.

**632. Fiction.** Representations of imagined events; philosophers ask how we can respond emotionally to what we know to be unreal.

**633. Paradox of Fiction.** The puzzle of why we feel real emotions for characters we know do not exist.

**634. Paradox of Tragedy.** The puzzle of why we take pleasure in works that depict painful events.

**635. Make-Believe.** Walton's account of fiction as props in games of imagination.

**636. Music.** The art of sound; philosophers ask how it expresses emotion and what its meaning consists in.

**637. Aesthetics of Nature.** The study of the appreciation of natural environments, in contrast with art.

**638. Environmental Aesthetics.** The study of how we perceive and value built and natural surroundings.

**639. Everyday Aesthetics.** The study of aesthetic qualities in ordinary life beyond the arts.

**640. Kitsch.** Sentimental, formulaic art that flatters its audience, which critics such as Greenberg and Kundera disdained.

**641. Aura.** Benjamin's term for the unique presence of an original artwork, which mechanical reproduction erodes.

**642. Culture Industry.** Adorno and Horkheimer's critique of mass-produced culture as a means of social control.

**643. Aesthetic Education.** Schiller's view that experience of beauty can cultivate freedom and moral character.

**644. Aesthetic Value.** The goodness of a thing as an object of aesthetic regard, and the question of whether it can be separated from moral and cognitive value.

**645. Moralism and Autonomism.** The debate over whether an artwork's moral qualities affect its aesthetic merit.

**646. Gaze.** The way in which viewing is structured by power, as in the male gaze of feminist film theory.

**647. Postmodernism in Art.** The rejection of grand narratives and the blurring of high and low, with play and pastiche.

**648. Simulacra.** Baudrillard's term for copies without originals, in a culture saturated with signs and images.

**649. Hyperreality.** Baudrillard's idea that models and images come to replace reality in advanced media culture.

**650. Sensus Communis.** The shared sense on which Kant based the demand that others agree with one's judgment of taste.

**651. Wabi-Sabi.** The Japanese aesthetic that finds beauty in impermanence and imperfection.

**652. Mono no Aware.** The Japanese sensitivity to the transience of things, a gentle sadness at their passing.

**653. Yūgen.** The Japanese aesthetic of subtle, profound mystery suggested but not stated.

**654. Rasa.** The Indian theory of aesthetic flavour, the emotional essence evoked in an audience by drama and poetry.


## 9. Philosophy of Science

**655. Scientific Method.** The set of practices by which science forms and tests theories; philosophers doubt whether any single method exists.

**656. Demarcation Problem.** The question of what separates science from pseudoscience and metaphysics.

**657. Falsifiability.** Popper's criterion that a theory is scientific only if observation could in principle refute it.

**658. Conjectures and Refutations.** Popper's picture of science as bold hypotheses tested by attempts to disprove them.

**659. Hypothetico-Deductive Method.** The method of deriving testable predictions from a hypothesis and checking them.

**660. Confirmation.** The relation by which evidence supports a hypothesis, analysed in logical, probabilistic and holistic terms.

**661. Raven Paradox.** Hempel's puzzle that a white shoe seems to confirm "all ravens are black".

**662. Grue Paradox.** Goodman's new riddle of induction, showing that evidence fits unnatural predicates as well as natural ones.

**663. Inference to the Best Explanation.** The principle that we should accept the hypothesis that best explains the evidence.

**664. Scientific Explanation.** The question of what it is to explain a phenomenon, whether by laws, causes or unification.

**665. Covering Law Model.** Hempel and Oppenheim's view that explanation is the deduction of a phenomenon from laws and conditions.

**666. Causal Explanation.** The view that to explain an event is to cite its causes, which many take to be the heart of explanation.

**667. Scientific Realism.** The view that mature scientific theories are approximately true and that their unobservable entities exist.

**668. Instrumentalism.** The view that scientific theories are tools for prediction and not literal descriptions of reality.

**669. Constructive Empiricism.** Van Fraassen's view that science aims at empirical adequacy, not truth about unobservables.

**670. No-Miracles Argument.** Putnam's argument that realism is the only view that does not make the success of science a miracle.

**671. Pessimistic Meta-Induction.** The argument that since past successful theories turned out false, current ones probably will too.

**672. Underdetermination of Theory.** The thesis that evidence cannot single out one theory among empirically equivalent rivals.

**673. Observable and Unobservable.** The distinction between what can be directly detected and what cannot, central to realism debates.

**674. Theory-Ladenness of Observation.** The thesis that observation is shaped by prior theory, which weakens any neutral foundation.

**675. Paradigm.** Kuhn's term for the framework of assumptions, methods and exemplars that guides a scientific community.

**676. Normal Science.** Kuhn's term for puzzle-solving work within an accepted paradigm.

**677. Scientific Revolution.** Kuhn's account of episodes in which an old paradigm is replaced by an incompatible one.

**678. Incommensurability.** Kuhn's and Feyerabend's thesis that rival paradigms lack a neutral standard of comparison.

**679. Research Programmes.** Lakatos's view of science as competing series of theories sharing a hard core and protective belt.

**680. Epistemological Anarchism.** Feyerabend's claim that no fixed method governs scientific progress, so "anything goes".

**681. Sociology of Scientific Knowledge.** The approach that explains scientific beliefs by social factors, as in the Strong Programme.

**682. Scientific Progress.** The question of whether and how science advances, by truth, problem-solving or empirical success.

**683. Verisimilitude.** Popper's notion of closeness to the truth, an attempt to say how theories can be better without being true.

**684. Laws of Nature in Science.** The role of general regularities in explanation and prediction, and the question of what makes them laws.

**685. Ceteris Paribus Laws.** Laws that hold only when other things are equal, common in the special sciences.

**686. Reductionism in Science.** The view that higher-level theories can be explained by more fundamental ones.

**687. Unity of Science.** The positivist ideal of a single science with all the special sciences reduced to physics.

**688. Disunity of Science.** The view that the sciences are a patchwork of methods and theories without a deep unity.

**689. Models.** Idealised representations used in science, whose relation to reality raises questions about truth and understanding.

**690. Idealization.** The deliberate simplification of a model, such as frictionless planes, that science uses to gain understanding.

**691. Thought Experiments in Science.** Imagined scenarios, as in Galileo and Einstein, used to reason about nature without experiment.

**692. Statistical Inference.** Methods for drawing conclusions from data, with frequentist and Bayesian schools in dispute.

**693. Philosophy of Physics.** The study of the conceptual foundations of physical theories, including space, time and quantum mechanics.

**694. Measurement Problem.** The puzzle in quantum mechanics of why a system in superposition yields a definite result on measurement.

**695. Interpretations of Quantum Mechanics.** Rival accounts of what quantum theory says about reality, including Copenhagen, many-worlds and pilot-wave.

**696. Many-Worlds Interpretation.** Everett's view that every quantum outcome occurs in a branching universe.

**697. Copenhagen Interpretation.** Bohr and Heisenberg's view that quantum theory describes the results of measurement and not an underlying reality.

**698. Wave-Particle Duality.** The finding that quantum entities show both wave and particle behaviour depending on how they are observed.

**699. Non-Locality.** The feature of quantum entanglement in which distant systems show correlations that no local account can explain.

**700. Philosophy of Biology.** The study of conceptual issues in evolution, genetics and ecology.

**701. Natural Selection.** Darwin's mechanism of evolutionary change through differential survival and reproduction.

**702. Adaptationism.** The view that traits are mainly to be explained as adaptations shaped by selection.

**703. Species Problem.** The difficulty of defining what a species is and whether species are natural kinds or individuals.

**704. Function.** The idea that parts of organisms have functions, analysed by selected effects or by causal role.

**705. Levels of Selection.** The debate over whether selection acts on genes, organisms, groups or all of these.

**706. Reductionism in Biology.** The view that biological phenomena can be explained by molecular and chemical processes.

**707. Vitalism.** The discredited view that living things contain a non-physical vital force.

**708. Philosophy of Psychology.** The study of the concepts and methods of the sciences of mind and behaviour.

**709. Philosophy of Social Science.** The study of whether the social sciences differ from the natural sciences in their methods and aims.

**710. Methodological Individualism.** The thesis that social phenomena should be explained by the actions of individuals.

**711. Holism in Social Science.** The view that social wholes have properties not reducible to individuals.

**712. Verstehen.** The interpretive understanding of human action from within, which Dilthey and Weber contrasted with natural explanation.

**713. Positivism.** The view that genuine knowledge derives from observation and logic, associated with Comte and the Vienna Circle.

**714. Values in Science.** The question of whether social and ethical values legitimately influence scientific reasoning.

**715. Ockham's Razor.** The principle that one should not multiply entities beyond necessity, used as a guide to theory choice.

**716. Simplicity.** A virtue of theories whose epistemic standing is debated: whether simpler theories are likelier to be true.

**717. Anthropic Principle.** The idea that observations of the universe must be compatible with the existence of observers.

**718. Fine-Tuning.** The observation that physical constants appear tuned for life, which is used in arguments for design or for multiple universes.

**719. Multiverse.** The hypothesis that our universe is one of many, proposed to explain fine-tuning.

**720. Arrow of Time.** The asymmetry between past and future, usually traced to the increase of entropy.

**721. Entropy.** A measure of disorder whose tendency to increase underlies the direction of time.

**722. Laplace's Demon.** The imagined intellect that could predict the future from complete knowledge of the present, an icon of determinism.


## 10. Philosophy of Religion

**723. God.** The supreme being of theism, conceived as all-powerful, all-knowing and perfectly good, whose existence and nature are the core questions of philosophical theology.

**724. Theism.** The belief in a personal God who created and sustains the world.

**725. Atheism.** The position that there is no God, or the lack of belief in one.

**726. Agnosticism.** The view that the existence of God is unknown or unknowable, a term coined by T. H. Huxley.

**727. Pantheism.** The view that God and the universe are identical, as in Spinoza.

**728. Panentheism.** The view that the world is in God, though God is also more than the world.

**729. Deism.** The view that God created the world but does not intervene in it, popular in the Enlightenment.

**730. Ontological Argument.** Anselm's argument that God, being that than which nothing greater can be conceived, must exist in reality as well as in thought.

**731. Cosmological Argument.** The argument from the existence of the world, or its contingency or beginning, to a first cause or necessary being.

**732. Kalam Argument.** The argument that the universe began to exist and so must have a cause, developed by medieval Islamic thinkers.

**733. Teleological Argument.** The argument from the order or design of the world to an intelligent designer, as in Paley's watch.

**734. Moral Argument.** The argument that objective morality, or the possibility of moral life, requires God.

**735. Five Ways.** Aquinas's five arguments for God's existence from motion, causation, contingency, degrees of perfection and design.

**736. Problem of Evil.** The challenge that the existence of evil and suffering is incompatible with an all-powerful, all-good God.

**737. Theodicy.** An attempt to justify God's goodness in the face of evil, as in Leibniz's claim that this is the best of all possible worlds.

**738. Free Will Defence.** The reply that God allowed evil in order to grant free will, which is a great good, as developed by Augustine and Plantinga.

**739. Natural and Moral Evil.** The distinction between suffering caused by nature and suffering caused by human wrongdoing.

**740. Divine Attributes.** Properties traditionally ascribed to God, including omnipotence, omniscience and omnibenevolence.

**741. Omnipotence.** The attribute of unlimited power, and the puzzles about whether God can do the logically impossible.

**742. Omniscience.** Complete knowledge, which raises questions about how it fits with human freedom.

**743. Divine Foreknowledge and Free Will.** The puzzle of whether God's knowledge of the future leaves us free, which Boethius and others addressed.

**744. Divine Simplicity.** The doctrine that God has no parts and is identical with his attributes.

**745. Eternity.** God's relation to time, either everlasting duration or timeless existence outside time.

**746. Divine Hiddenness.** Schellenberg's argument that a loving God would make his existence evident, so that reasonable non-belief counts against God.

**747. Faith and Reason.** The relation between religious belief and rational inquiry, held by turns to be harmonious or opposed.

**748. Fideism.** The view that faith does not depend on reason and may be opposed to it, as in Kierkegaard.

**749. Leap of Faith.** Kierkegaard's idea that religious commitment requires a passionate leap beyond what reason can justify.

**750. Teleological Suspension of the Ethical.** Kierkegaard's reading of Abraham's willingness to sacrifice Isaac, in which faith overrides ethical duty.

**751. Religious Experience.** Direct encounters with the divine, studied by James as evidence for religion.

**752. Mysticism.** Experience of union with ultimate reality, found across traditions and raising questions of ineffability.

**753. Miracles.** Events that violate natural law, which Hume argued can never be believed on the basis of testimony.

**754. Religious Pluralism.** The view of Hick that the great religions are different responses to one ultimate reality.

**755. Exclusivism and Inclusivism.** Views on whether only one religion is true or whether others share in truth to some degree.

**756. Afterlife.** The continued existence of persons after death, a hope in many religions and a puzzle for personal identity.

**757. Immortality of the Soul.** Plato's thesis that the soul is imperishable and survives the death of the body.

**758. Resurrection.** The Christian belief that the dead will be raised bodily, which raises puzzles of identity.

**759. Religious Language.** The problem of how words can describe a transcendent God, answered by analogy, symbol or via negativa.

**760. Negative Theology.** The approach of describing God only by what God is not, to avoid limiting the divine.

**761. Via Negativa.** The path of approaching God by negation, a method of Pseudo-Dionysius and Maimonides.

**762. Secularization.** The decline of the social and cultural authority of religion in modern societies.

**763. Death of God.** Nietzsche's proclamation that belief in God has become untenable, and that the foundations of Western morality are shaken.

**764. Projection Theory of Religion.** Feuerbach's view that God is a projection of human qualities onto an imagined being.

**765. Opium of the People.** Marx's description of religion as a consoling illusion that masks social suffering.

**766. Reformed Epistemology and Religion.** Plantinga's defence of belief in God as rational without argument.

**767. Apophatic Mysticism.** The tradition of unknowing in which the divine is approached by silence and negation.

**768. Predestination.** The doctrine that God has determined in advance who will be saved, central to Augustine and Calvin.

**769. Grace.** In Christian thought, the unmerited gift of divine favour, a central matter in debates about salvation and works.

**770. Original Sin.** The Christian doctrine that humanity inherits a sinful condition from Adam's fall.

**771. Natural Theology.** The attempt to establish truths about God by reason alone, without appeal to revelation.

**772. Revelation.** Disclosure of divine truth to humanity, in contrast with knowledge by reason alone.

**773. Religious Tolerance.** The principle that different faiths may coexist peacefully, defended by Locke, Voltaire and Mendelssohn.

**774. Philosophy of Religion.** The study of the concepts and arguments of religious belief, covering God, evil, faith and the afterlife.

**775. Scholasticism.** The medieval method and tradition of Aquinas, Anselm and Scotus that used logical analysis and disputation to reconcile Christian doctrine with Aristotelian philosophy.


## 11. Existentialism, Phenomenology and Continental Philosophy

**776. Existentialism.** The movement, from Kierkegaard through Sartre, that begins from individual existence, freedom and choice in a world without given meaning.

**777. Authenticity.** Living in accord with one's own choices and being, in contrast to conforming to the crowd, a theme in Heidegger and Sartre.

**778. Bad Faith.** Sartre's term for self-deception in which one denies one's freedom by pretending to be a fixed thing.

**779. Absurd.** Camus's term for the clash between our demand for meaning and the silence of the universe.

**780. Revolt.** Camus's response to the absurd: to live in defiance, without hope or despair.

**781. Myth of Sisyphus.** Camus's essay that treats Sisyphus condemned to roll a rock as the image of absurd life, which one must imagine happy.

**782. Anguish.** In Kierkegaard and Sartre, the dizzying awareness of one's freedom and responsibility.

**783. Angst.** Heidegger's term for the basic mood in which the nothingness of existence is disclosed.

**784. Being-for-Itself.** Sartre's term for consciousness, which is free and lacks fixed nature, as opposed to being-in-itself.

**785. Being-in-Itself.** Sartre's term for the solid, self-identical being of things, which simply is.

**786. Condemned to Be Free.** Sartre's thesis that human beings cannot escape freedom and must choose even when they refuse to.

**787. The Look.** Sartre's analysis of how being seen by another turns one into an object and reveals shame.

**788. Facticity.** The given circumstances of one's existence, such as body, past and situation, which one cannot choose.

**789. Transcendence.** The human capacity to go beyond what is given by projecting oneself toward possibilities.

**790. Dasein.** Heidegger's term for the human mode of being, the being for whom being is an issue.

**791. Being-in-the-World.** Heidegger's thesis that we are always already engaged with a world of tools and others, not detached subjects.

**792. Being-toward-Death.** Heidegger's idea that confronting one's own mortality is the key to authentic existence.

**793. Thrownness.** Heidegger's term for the fact that we find ourselves already in a world not of our choosing.

**794. Ready-to-Hand.** Heidegger's term for equipment as used in practice, as opposed to present-at-hand objects observed theoretically.

**795. The They.** Heidegger's das Man, the anonymous public norms that govern everyday life and dissolve individuality.

**796. Ontological Difference.** Heidegger's distinction between beings and the Being by which they are, which philosophy has forgotten.

**797. Care.** Heidegger's name for the basic structure of Dasein as concerned about its own existence.

**798. Phenomenology.** Husserl's study of the structures of experience as they appear to consciousness.

**799. Epoché.** Husserl's bracketing of assumptions about the existence of the world in order to describe experience as it is given.

**800. Intentionality in Phenomenology.** Husserl's thesis that all consciousness is consciousness of something.

**801. Lifeworld.** Husserl's term for the pre-theoretical world of lived experience that underlies scientific abstraction.

**802. Embodiment.** Merleau-Ponty's view that the lived body is the subject of perception and not an object among others.

**803. Lived Body.** The body as experienced from within, in contrast with the body as a physical object.

**804. Intersubjectivity.** The shared structure of experience among subjects, important in Husserl and Schutz.

**805. Alterity.** Otherness; Levinas held that the encounter with the Other, not the self, grounds ethics.

**806. Face of the Other.** Levinas's image of the other person's face, which commands responsibility before any theory.

**807. Ethics as First Philosophy.** Levinas's thesis that responsibility for the Other precedes ontology.

**808. Leap and Stages of Existence.** Kierkegaard's account of aesthetic, ethical and religious ways of life, with transitions through leaps.

**809. Subjectivity is Truth.** Kierkegaard's claim that in matters of existence, the manner of holding a belief matters more than its content.

**810. Eternal Return.** Nietzsche's thought experiment of living one's life again infinitely, as a test of affirming life.

**811. Amor Fati.** Nietzsche's love of fate, the willingness to affirm everything that happens, even suffering.

**812. Übermensch.** Nietzsche's overman, who creates new values after the death of God.

**813. Perspectivism.** Nietzsche's view that all knowledge is from a perspective and that no view from nowhere exists.

**814. Nihilism.** The loss of belief in meaning or value, which Nietzsche saw as the crisis of modernity.

**815. Duration.** Bergson's term for time as lived, a continuous flow that spatialised clock time distorts.

**816. Élan Vital.** Bergson's vital impetus, a creative force in evolution.

**817. Spirit.** Hegel's Geist, the self-developing reality that comes to know itself through history.

**818. Phenomenology of Spirit.** Hegel's 1807 account of consciousness developing through stages to absolute knowing.

**819. Master-Slave Dialectic.** Hegel's analysis of the struggle for recognition in which the dependent servant achieves self-consciousness through work.

**820. Aufhebung.** Hegel's term for sublation: the cancelling, preserving and lifting up of a stage in a higher unity.

**821. Cunning of Reason.** Hegel's idea that history advances reason's ends through the passions and self-interested actions of individuals.

**822. Will.** Schopenhauer's blind, striving reality underlying appearance, the source of suffering.

**823. Pessimism.** Schopenhauer's view that existence is dominated by suffering and that the best response is denial of the will.

**824. Critical Philosophy.** Kant's project of examining the limits and conditions of reason before applying it.

**825. Copernican Revolution in Philosophy.** Kant's turn from asking how the mind conforms to objects to asking how objects conform to the mind.

**826. Categories of Understanding.** Kant's twelve pure concepts, such as causality and substance, by which the mind structures experience.

**827. Antinomies.** Kant's pairs of contradictory conclusions reason falls into when it applies its categories beyond experience.

**828. Critique of Pure Reason.** Kant's 1781 work examining what reason can know apart from experience and where it oversteps its limits.

**829. Enlightenment.** Kant's "emergence from self-incurred immaturity" through daring to use one's own reason.

**830. German Idealism.** The movement of Fichte, Schelling and Hegel that developed Kant's philosophy into systems of the self-determining mind.

**831. Romanticism.** The movement valuing feeling, nature and creative individuality against Enlightenment rationalism.

**832. Pragmatism.** The American school of Peirce, James and Dewey that tests ideas by their practical consequences.

**833. Pragmatic Maxim.** Peirce's rule that the meaning of a concept lies in its conceivable practical effects.

**834. Neopragmatism.** Rorty's and Brandom's revival of pragmatism that rejects the idea of the mind as a mirror of nature.

**835. Psychoanalysis.** Freud's theory of the unconscious, which challenged the Cartesian transparency of mind.

**836. Unconscious.** The mental processes beyond awareness that shape thought and behaviour, according to Freud.

**837. Dialectic of Enlightenment.** Adorno and Horkheimer's argument that Enlightenment reason reverts into domination.

**838. One-Dimensional Man.** Marcuse's thesis that advanced industrial society suppresses critical thought.

**839. Society of the Spectacle.** Debord's thesis that modern life is mediated by images and commodities.

**840. Rhizome.** Deleuze and Guattari's image of non-hierarchical, interconnected thought contrasted with the tree.

**841. Archaeology of Knowledge.** Foucault's method of uncovering the rules governing what can be said in an historical period.

**842. Postmodernism.** The skepticism toward grand narratives and universal truths, associated with Lyotard.

**843. Incredulity toward Metanarratives.** Lyotard's definition of the postmodern condition as distrust of grand stories, such as progress or emancipation, that claim to legitimise all knowledge.

**844. Poststructuralism.** The movement after structuralism, including Derrida, Foucault and Deleuze, that stresses instability of meaning and structure.

**845. Speculative Realism.** A recent movement that rejects the primacy of human access to the world over reality independent of thought.


## 12. Action, Free Will, Identity and the Examined Life

**846. Free Will.** The capacity of agents to choose among alternatives and to be the source of their actions, the subject of one of philosophy's oldest debates.

**847. Determinism.** The thesis that every event is necessitated by prior events and the laws of nature.

**848. Compatibilism.** The view that free will is compatible with determinism, defended by Hume, Frankfurt and Dennett.

**849. Incompatibilism.** The view that free will and determinism cannot both be true.

**850. Libertarianism about Free Will.** The incompatibilist view that we have free will and that determinism is false.

**851. Hard Determinism.** The view that determinism is true and that therefore there is no free will.

**852. Hard Incompatibilism.** Pereboom's view that free will, in the sense required for desert, is incompatible with both determinism and indeterminism.

**853. Consequence Argument.** Van Inwagen's argument that if determinism is true, our acts are consequences of facts we cannot control.

**854. Principle of Alternate Possibilities.** The principle that a person is responsible only if they could have done otherwise.

**855. Frankfurt Cases.** Frankfurt's examples of agents who are responsible though they could not have done otherwise, challenging the principle of alternate possibilities.

**856. Hierarchical Theory of Free Will.** Frankfurt's view that freedom involves having the will one wants to have, through second-order desires.

**857. Reasons-Responsiveness.** The view that an agent is free when they respond appropriately to reasons, as in Fischer and Ravizza.

**858. Agent Causation.** The view that free actions are caused directly by agents, not by prior events.

**859. Event-Causal Libertarianism.** The view that free actions are undetermined events caused by the agent's mental states.

**860. Luck Objection.** The challenge that indeterministic choices seem random and hence not free.

**861. Manipulation Argument.** Pereboom's argument that a manipulated agent is unfree, and that determinism is relevantly like manipulation.

**862. Basic Argument.** Galen Strawson's argument that no one can be ultimately responsible for who they are and so for what they do.

**863. Reactive Attitudes.** Strawson's idea that resentment and gratitude are natural responses that underlie our practice of holding responsible.

**864. Illusionism about Free Will.** The view that belief in free will is an illusion that may nonetheless be useful.

**865. Neuroscience and Free Will.** The issue raised by Libet's experiments about whether unconscious brain activity precedes conscious decisions.

**866. Fatalism.** The view that what will happen is fixed regardless of what we do.

**867. Action.** What agents do, as opposed to what merely happens to them; philosophers ask what distinguishes the two.

**868. Intention.** The mental state of aiming to act, which Anscombe distinguished from mere prediction.

**869. Reasons and Causes.** The debate over whether an agent's reasons for acting are also causes of the action, as Davidson maintained.

**870. Practical Reason.** Reasoning about what to do, as opposed to reasoning about what to believe.

**871. Intention and Foresight.** The distinction between what an agent aims at and what they merely foresee as a side effect.

**872. Weakness of Will.** Acting against one's own considered judgment of what is best.

**873. Self-Control.** The capacity to govern one's impulses in line with one's values.

**874. Self-Deception.** Believing something while at some level knowing it to be false, which poses puzzles about the unity of the mind.

**875. Moral Agency.** The capacity to act on moral reasons and to be held accountable.

**876. Collective Intentionality.** The capacity of groups to share intentions and act together, studied by Searle and Bratman.

**877. Joint Action.** Action performed by several agents together, analysed in terms of shared intentions.

**878. Personal Identity.** The question of what makes a person at one time the same as a person at another.

**879. Psychological Continuity.** The view, from Locke, that personal identity consists in the continuity of memory and character.

**880. Bodily Criterion.** The view that personal identity consists in the continuation of the same body or brain.

**881. Animalism.** The view that we are human animals and that personal identity is that of an organism.

**882. Teletransportation.** Parfit's thought experiment of being scanned and recreated elsewhere, to test what matters in survival.

**883. Fission.** Cases in which a person divides into two, used by Parfit to argue that identity is not what matters.

**884. What Matters in Survival.** Parfit's thesis that psychological continuity and connectedness, not identity, is what matters.

**885. Reductionism about Persons.** Parfit's view that a person's existence consists in other facts such as brain events and mental states.

**886. Narrative Identity.** The view that a person's identity is formed by the story they tell about their life.

**887. Soul.** The immaterial principle of life or mind in Plato, Aristotle and Christian thought, whose existence is disputed.

**888. Death.** The end of life, whose badness Epicurus denied and Nagel defended as a deprivation.

**889. Epicurus on Death.** The view that death is nothing to us, since when we exist death is not, and when death exists we are not.

**890. Deprivation Account of Death.** Nagel's view that death is bad because it deprives us of the goods of continued life.

**891. Meaning of Life.** The question of whether life has a purpose or significance, answered by religion, humanism and nihilism.

**892. Happiness.** The good life for a person, understood as pleasure, satisfaction or flourishing.

**893. Stoic Acceptance.** The Stoic view that peace comes from accepting what is outside our control and attending to what is within it.

**894. Dichotomy of Control.** Epictetus's distinction between what is up to us, such as judgments, and what is not.

**895. Ataraxia.** The Hellenistic ideal of tranquillity, freedom from disturbance, sought by Epicureans and skeptics.

**896. Apatheia.** The Stoic ideal of freedom from destructive passions.

**897. Cynicism.** The ancient school, from Diogenes, that rejected convention and sought a life in accord with nature.

**898. Epicureanism.** The philosophy of Epicurus that sought tranquil pleasure through modest desires and friendship.

**899. Stoicism.** The school of Zeno, Epictetus, Seneca and Marcus Aurelius that taught virtue as the sole good and life in accord with reason.

**900. Logos.** In Heraclitus and the Stoics, the rational order of the cosmos, also word and reason.

**901. Love.** Philosophers analyse love as desire for the beautiful (Plato), union (Aristophanes), valuing, or concern for the beloved's good.

**902. Platonic Love.** Plato's ascent from love of a body to love of Beauty itself, as in the Symposium.


## 13. Non-Western Traditions

**903. Dharma.** In Indian thought, the order of the cosmos and the duty and right conduct that sustains it, with specific meanings in Hindu, Buddhist and Jain traditions.

**904. Karma.** The doctrine that intentional actions have moral consequences that shape one's future, including later rebirths.

**905. Samsara.** The cycle of birth, death and rebirth that Indian traditions seek to escape.

**906. Moksha.** Liberation from the cycle of rebirth, the supreme goal in many Indian traditions.

**907. Atman.** The self or soul, which the Upanishads identify with the ultimate reality.

**908. Brahman.** The ultimate, unchanging reality underlying the world in Vedic and Vedantic thought.

**909. Maya.** In Advaita Vedanta, the power by which the one reality appears as a world of multiplicity.

**910. Advaita Vedanta.** Shankara's non-dualist school holding that Atman and Brahman are one and that the world of difference is appearance.

**911. Vishishtadvaita.** Ramanuja's qualified non-dualism, in which souls and world are real but dependent parts of Brahman.

**912. Dvaita Vedanta.** Madhva's dualism, which holds that God and individual souls are eternally distinct.

**913. Upanishads.** The philosophical texts of the Vedic tradition that explore the self, Brahman and liberation.

**914. Rta.** The Vedic idea of cosmic order that governs nature and ritual and anticipates dharma.

**915. Samkhya.** The dualist school that explains the world through two ultimate principles, spirit and nature.

**916. Purusha and Prakriti.** Samkhya's dual principles of conscious spirit and unconscious nature, whose entanglement causes suffering.

**917. Yoga Philosophy.** Patanjali's system of disciplined practice leading to the cessation of mental fluctuation and the isolation of the self.

**918. Nyaya.** The Indian school of logic and epistemology that analyses the means of valid knowledge and the structure of debate.

**919. Pramana.** The accepted means of valid knowledge in Indian thought, such as perception, inference and testimony.

**920. Vaisheshika.** The atomist school that classifies reality into categories such as substance, quality and action.

**921. Mimamsa.** The school devoted to interpreting Vedic ritual, holding the Vedas to be eternal and authorless.

**922. Anekantavada.** The Jain doctrine of many-sidedness, that reality is too complex to be captured by any single viewpoint.

**923. Syadvada.** The Jain doctrine that every statement is true only in some respect, expressed with a qualifying "in some way".

**924. Ahimsa.** Non-violence toward all living beings, a central virtue in Jainism, Buddhism and Hinduism, later used by Gandhi.

**925. Four Noble Truths.** The Buddha's teaching that life involves suffering, which has a cause, can cease, and has a path to its end.

**926. Noble Eightfold Path.** The Buddhist path of right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness and concentration.

**927. Dependent Origination.** The Buddhist teaching that all phenomena arise in dependence on conditions and have no independent existence.

**928. Dukkha.** The Buddhist term for suffering or unsatisfactoriness, the characteristic of conditioned existence.

**929. Impermanence.** The Buddhist doctrine that all conditioned things are transient and in constant change.

**930. Nirvana.** The Buddhist goal of the extinction of craving and suffering, and release from rebirth.

**931. Middle Way.** The Buddha's path between self-indulgence and extreme asceticism, and between eternalism and annihilationism.

**932. Emptiness.** Nagarjuna's doctrine that all things lack inherent nature and exist only in dependence on other things.

**933. Madhyamaka.** Nagarjuna's school that develops the doctrine of emptiness through a critique of all fixed views.

**934. Two Truths.** The Buddhist distinction between conventional truth and ultimate truth, as interpreted by Madhyamaka.

**935. Yogacara.** The Buddhist school that teaches that experience is structured by consciousness and that the world is mind-dependent.

**936. Buddha-Nature.** The Mahayana teaching that all beings have the potential for awakening.

**937. Bodhisattva.** One who vows to attain awakening for the sake of all beings and postpones final release to help them.

**938. Compassion.** The Buddhist virtue of karuna, wishing to relieve the suffering of others, joined to wisdom.

**939. Mindfulness.** The Buddhist practice of attentive awareness of the present moment as a path to insight.

**940. Abhidharma.** The systematic Buddhist analysis of experience into basic constituents called dharmas.

**941. Buddhist Logic.** Dignaga's and Dharmakirti's theory of knowledge, which treats perception and inference as the only valid means.

**942. Catuskoti.** The four-cornered logic of affirming, denying, both and neither, used in Buddhist argument.

**943. Zen.** The Buddhist school, from Chan, that emphasises direct insight through meditation and beyond doctrinal study.

**944. Satori.** In Zen, a sudden flash of awakening or insight into one's true nature.

**945. Koan.** A paradoxical riddle used in Zen to break the grip of discursive thought.

**946. Kyoto School.** The twentieth-century Japanese philosophers, including Nishida and Nishitani, who combined Zen with Western philosophy.

**947. Absolute Nothingness.** Nishida's and Nishitani's idea of a groundless, unobjectifiable basis of reality that makes all things possible.

**948. Being-Time.** Dogen's thesis that being and time are inseparable and that each moment is complete in itself.

**949. Dao.** The Way, the fundamental pattern of nature and conduct, in Daoism an ineffable source of all things and in Confucianism the right path of life.

**950. Wu Wei.** Non-forcing action, the Daoist ideal of acting in harmony with the natural course of things.

**951. Yin and Yang.** The pair of complementary, interdependent forces whose interplay is seen to generate change in Chinese cosmology.

**952. Qi.** The vital energy or breath that animates the cosmos and the body in Chinese thought.

**953. Ziran.** Spontaneity or naturalness, the self-so quality of the Dao and of things that follow it.

**954. Daodejing.** The classic ascribed to Laozi, which teaches humility, simplicity and yielding in accord with the Dao.

**955. Butterfly Dream.** Zhuangzi's story of dreaming he was a butterfly, which questions the line between dream and waking and between self and other.

**956. Free and Easy Wandering.** Zhuangzi's ideal of spontaneous freedom that transcends conventional distinctions.

**957. Confucianism.** The tradition of Confucius that stresses moral cultivation, social roles, ritual and benevolent government.

**958. Ren.** The Confucian virtue of humaneness or benevolence, the root of ethical life.

**959. Li.** Ritual propriety, the forms of conduct through which Confucians cultivate character and social harmony.

**960. Yi.** Righteousness, the Confucian sense of doing what is appropriate in each situation.

**961. Filial Piety.** The Confucian virtue of respect and care for parents and ancestors, the root of other virtues.

**962. Junzi.** The exemplary person of Confucian ethics, who cultivates virtue and leads by example.

**963. Rectification of Names.** Confucius's thesis that social order requires words to match the roles and realities they name.

**964. Mandate of Heaven.** The Chinese doctrine that rulers hold power by Heaven's approval, which is lost through misrule.

**965. Mencius on Human Nature.** Mencius's view that human nature is good, with moral sprouts that must be nurtured.

**966. Xunzi on Human Nature.** Xunzi's view that human nature is bad and must be reshaped by ritual and education.

**967. Impartial Care.** The Mohist doctrine that we should care for all people equally, as an alternative to Confucian graded love.

**968. Legalism.** The school of Han Feizi that holds that order is maintained by strict law, punishment and the ruler's control.

**969. School of Names.** The Chinese logicians, including Gongsun Long, who analysed language, paradox and the relation between names and things.

**970. Neo-Confucianism.** The Song and Ming revival of Confucianism that integrated metaphysics from Buddhism and Daoism.

**971. Principle and Qi.** Zhu Xi's pairing of li, the patterning principle, with qi, the material energy that realises it.

**972. Unity of Knowledge and Action.** Wang Yangming's thesis that true knowledge of the good is inseparable from acting on it.

**973. Five Phases.** The Chinese system of wood, fire, earth, metal and water as interacting processes of change.

**974. Harmony.** In Chinese thought, the ideal of diverse elements cooperating without being made the same.

**975. Tianxia.** "All under heaven", the Chinese idea of a world order that is moral and universal, not merely political.

**976. Ma.** The Japanese notion of meaningful interval or negative space, central to aesthetics and thought.

**977. Bushido.** The code of the samurai, emphasising loyalty, honour and self-discipline.

**978. Falsafa.** The tradition of Islamic philosophy that took up and developed Greek thought in Arabic.

**979. Floating Man.** Avicenna's thought experiment of a suspended man who is aware of himself without sensing his body, suggesting the soul's independence.

**980. Necessary Existent.** Avicenna's argument that a being whose essence entails its existence must ground all contingent things.

**981. Unity of the Intellect.** Averroes's disputed doctrine that the active intellect is one for all humanity.

**982. Incoherence of the Philosophers.** Al-Ghazali's attack on the Aristotelian philosophers, which targeted their claims on causation and the eternity of the world.

**983. Kalam.** The Islamic tradition of rational theology that defends doctrine by argument.

**984. Mutazila.** The rationalist school of Islamic theology holding that reason can know good and evil and that God is just.

**985. Sufism.** The mystical dimension of Islam that seeks direct experience of God through love and discipline.

**986. Fana.** The Sufi annihilation of the self in God.

**987. Illuminationism.** Suhrawardi's philosophy in which reality is a hierarchy of light, known by direct presence as well as by reasoning.

**988. Primacy of Existence.** Mulla Sadra's claim that existence, not essence, is fundamental and that being admits of degrees and motion.

**989. Virtuous City.** Al-Farabi's vision of a state ruled by a philosopher-prophet, modelled on Plato.

**990. Asabiyyah.** Ibn Khaldun's concept of group solidarity as the driving force in the rise and fall of dynasties.

**991. I and Thou.** Buber's distinction between the I-It relation of use and the I-Thou relation of mutual presence.

**992. Kabbalah.** The Jewish mystical tradition that interprets the divine as emanating through a structure of ten spheres.

**993. Ubuntu.** The southern African idea that a person is a person through other persons, grounding an ethics of community.

**994. Akan Concept of Person.** The Akan view of a person as a composite of elements, with an emphasis on community and character.

**995. Sage Philosophy.** The study by Odera Oruka of the reflective thought of traditional African sages.

**996. Négritude.** The movement of Césaire and Senghor that affirmed black identity and culture against colonial denigration.

**997. Ethnophilosophy.** The study of philosophical thought embodied in the shared beliefs and practices of African peoples, and the critique of it.

**998. Philosophy of Liberation.** Dussel's and others' Latin American philosophy that starts from the standpoint of the oppressed.

**999. Buen Vivir.** The Andean ideal of living well in harmony with community and nature.

**1000. Indigenous Relationality.** The view found across many Indigenous traditions that persons, land and other beings are bound in reciprocal relationships.
